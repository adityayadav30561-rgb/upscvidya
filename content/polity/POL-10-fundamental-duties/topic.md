# Fundamental Duties

Fundamental Duties are contained in **Part IVA**, which consists of a single article — **Article 51A**. They were added by the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** on the recommendation of the **Swaran Singh Committee**, and are inspired by the Constitution of the **erstwhile USSR**. No other major democratic constitution — the American included — carries a comparable list.

:::predict Before you read on — the Swaran Singh Committee recommended a certain number of duties. Did Parliament enact exactly that number?
No. The committee recommended **8** duties; the 42nd Amendment enacted **10**. The **86th Amendment Act, 2002** later added the 11th.
:::

## What the committee asked for, and what was refused

The Swaran Singh Committee (1976) went further than Part IVA finally did. Three of its recommendations were **not accepted**:

- a **penalty or punishment** for non-compliance with a duty;
- a bar on **any court questioning** such a penalty law;
- a duty to **pay taxes**.

That refusal is the reason Fundamental Duties look toothless on paper — the enforcement machinery the committee designed was dropped, and only the list survived.

## The eleven duties (Article 51A)

Every citizen shall —

- **(a)** abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag and the National Anthem;
- **(b)** cherish and follow the noble ideals that inspired the national freedom struggle;
- **(c)** uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India;
- **(d)** defend the country and render national service when called upon;
- **(e)** promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood transcending religious, linguistic, regional and sectional diversities, and renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women;
- **(f)** value and preserve the rich heritage of the composite culture;
- **(g)** protect and improve the **natural environment** — forests, lakes, rivers, wildlife — and have compassion for living creatures;
- **(h)** develop **scientific temper**, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform;
- **(i)** safeguard public property and abjure violence;
- **(j)** strive towards **excellence** in all spheres of individual and collective activity;
- **(k)** provide opportunities for education to a child or ward between **6 and 14 years** — added by the **86th Amendment, 2002**.

## Features worth memorising

- Some duties are **moral** (cherishing the ideals of the freedom struggle), others **civic** (respecting the Flag and Anthem).
- They apply **only to citizens** — foreigners are outside Article 51A, unlike Fundamental Rights such as Articles 14 and 21 which extend to all persons.
- They are **non-justiciable**: Article 51A itself prescribes no penalty and no court can enforce a duty directly.
- But Parliament **may** enforce a specific duty by ordinary law — and has. The **Verma Committee (1999)** catalogued the existing machinery: the *Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971*, the *Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955*, the *Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972*, the *Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980*, and section 123 of the *Representation of the People Act, 1951*.
- They are an **interpretive aid**: courts use them to test the reasonableness of a law. In **AIIMS Students Union v. AIIMS (2001)** the Supreme Court held that Fundamental Duties are as important as Fundamental Rights.

> [!exam] The favourite trap is the count at each stage: **8 recommended → 10 enacted (1976) → 11 today (2002)**.

## Criticism

The list has been attacked as vague and non-exhaustive, as carrying no legal sanction, and as superfluous — since a citizen would do these things anyway. Critics also argue it was misplaced: appending duties to Part IV (Directive Principles) reduced their value, and they should have followed Part III, immediately after the Fundamental Rights.

:::recall In one line — why do Fundamental Duties still matter in court if no court can enforce them?
They are **non-justiciable but interpretive**: a court may use Article 51A to judge whether a law is reasonable, and Parliament may back any single duty with an ordinary statute (as with the National Honour Act, 1971).
:::
