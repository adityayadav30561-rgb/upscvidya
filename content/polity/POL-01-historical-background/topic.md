# Historical Background

A trading company arrived in India in **1608** and, a century and a half later,
found itself governing millions of people. Nobody planned that. Everything in
this chapter follows from it: the British Parliament spent the next 174 years
improvising laws to control a merchant firm that had accidentally become a state,
and those improvised laws became the machinery of Indian government.

That is why this chapter matters far more than its "history" label suggests. The
Constitution of 1950 did not appear from nothing. Its federal structure, its
three legislative lists, its public service commissions, its emergency powers —
all were running in British India before independence. When you meet Article 356
later and it feels oddly familiar, it is because you met its ancestor here.

Two questions run through every Act below. Ask them each time: **who controls
India**, and **how much say do Indians get**? Every statute moves one or both.

:::predict Of all the colonial statutes in this chapter, one supplied the actual skeleton the Constituent Assembly built on. Which one, and what did it hand over?
The **Government of India Act, 1935**. Its three legislative lists, provincial autonomy, federal court and public service commissions passed into the Constitution almost intact — which is why it is the most heavily examined Act in this chapter.
:::

## How a trading company became a government

The East India Company was chartered by **Queen Elizabeth I in 1600** with a
monopoly on English trade to the East. It reached India in **1608**, and for over
150 years it did what trading companies do — bought, sold, and built fortified
warehouses called factories.

The turn came in **1765**. After defeating the combined forces of the Nawab of
Bengal, the Nawab of Awadh and the Mughal emperor at the **Battle of Buxar
(1764)**, the Company extracted from Emperor **Shah Alam II** the **diwani** of
**Bengal, Bihar and Orissa** — the right to collect revenue and administer civil
justice.

Understand why this single grant changed everything. Collecting revenue means
deciding who owes what, which means courts, records, police and an
administration. A body that collects taxes from a population *is* a government,
whatever its charter says. The Company now had an income independent of trade and
an empire it had never been authorised to acquire.

> [!note] Diwani = **revenue + civil justice**. Criminal justice (*nizamat*)
> formally stayed with the Nawab — a distinction examiners like, because it shows
> the Company ruled through Indian forms rather than replacing them outright.

By the 1770s the Company was near bankruptcy while its servants grew rich, and it
came to Parliament for a loan. Parliament used that leverage to start regulating
it. Company rule from here is a single story: **London tightening its grip**.

:::cloze
The Company's charter came from {{Queen Elizabeth I}} in the year {{1600}}, but it reached India only in {{1608}}.
The grant of {{diwani}} in 1765 covered Bengal, Bihar and Orissa and was made by the Mughal emperor {{Shah Alam II|Shah Alam}}.
:::

## The Company Rule (1773–1858)

### Regulating Act, 1773 — Parliament steps in

The first British law to control the Company, and important for three reasons: it
was the first step towards central administration, it recognised the Company's
political functions for the first time, and it laid the foundation of
centralisation in India.

- The Governor of Bengal became **Governor-General of Bengal**, assisted by an
  Executive Council of **four** members. **Lord Warren Hastings** was the first.
- Bombay and Madras were made **subordinate** to Bengal. Until now the three
  presidencies had been independent of one another.
- A **Supreme Court at Calcutta** was provided for — established in **1774** with
  one chief justice and three other judges.
- Company servants were barred from private trade and from accepting presents or
  bribes.
- The Court of Directors had to report its revenue, civil and military affairs to
  the British Government.

> [!warn] The Act *provided for* the Supreme Court in **1773**; the court was
> actually **established in 1774**. That one-year gap is a favourite question.

### Amending Act, 1781 — fixing the overreach

Also called the **Act of Settlement**. The Supreme Court had begun asserting
jurisdiction over almost everyone, including the government itself, so Parliament
pulled it back:

- The Governor-General and Council were **exempted** from its jurisdiction for
  official acts, as were Company servants.
- **Revenue matters** were placed outside its jurisdiction entirely.
- Its authority was confined to the inhabitants of **Calcutta**, and it was
  required to apply the defendant's **personal law** — Hindu law for Hindus,
  Mohammedan law for Muslims.
- Appeals from Provincial Courts went to the **Governor-General-in-Council**, not
  to the Supreme Court.

### Pitt's India Act, 1784 — the double government

Named for Prime Minister William Pitt, this Act split the Company in two:

- **Commercial** functions stayed with the **Court of Directors**.
- **Political** functions went to a new **Board of Control**, which supervised
  civil, military and revenue administration.

This arrangement — two bodies governing one territory — is the **double
government**, and it lasted until 1858. The Act also called the Company's
territories the "**British possessions in India**" for the first time, quietly
conceding that this was now an imperial holding, not a business.

### Act of 1786 and Charter Act, 1793

**Lord Cornwallis** agreed to serve as Governor-General on two conditions: power
to **override his council** in special cases, and appointment as
**Commander-in-Chief**. The **Act of 1786** granted both. The **Charter Act of
1793** then extended that override power to all future Governors-General and
Governors, renewed the Company's trade monopoly for **twenty more years**, and
provided that the Board of Control's staff be paid **out of Indian revenues**.

### Charter Act, 1813 — the monopoly cracks

- The Company's **trade monopoly in India ended**; Indian trade opened to all
  British merchants. But the monopoly over **tea and trade with China** was
  **retained**.
- The **sovereignty of the British Crown** over Company territories was asserted.
- **Christian missionaries** were permitted to enter India.
- Provision was made for the **spread of western education** — the first such
  legislative provision.

:::cloze
The Charter Act of {{1813}} ended the Company's Indian trade monopoly but kept its monopoly over {{tea}} and trade with {{China}}.
Pitt's India Act of {{1784}} created the {{Board of Control}} for political affairs, producing the system known as {{double government}}.
:::

### Charter Act, 1833 — the final step towards centralisation

The most consequential of the Charter Acts.

- The Governor-General of Bengal became **Governor-General of India**, holding
  all civil and military authority over every British territory in India.
  **Lord William Bentinck** was the first.
- Bombay and Madras **lost their legislative powers**. The Governor-General of
  India got exclusive legislative authority for all of British India.
- Laws made under earlier statutes were called **Regulations**; those under this
  Act were called **Acts**.
- The Company ceased to be a commercial body and became **purely administrative**,
  holding its territories "in trust for His Majesty".
- It **attempted** to introduce open competition for the civil services and
  declared that Indians should not be debarred from office — but the Court of
  Directors opposed it and the provision was **negated**.

### Charter Act, 1853 — a mini-Parliament appears

The last Charter Act, and a genuine constitutional landmark.

- It **separated the legislative and executive functions** of the
  Governor-General's Council for the first time, adding **six** legislative
  councillors. This created a separate **Indian (Central) Legislative Council**
  that functioned as a mini-Parliament using British parliamentary procedure.
  Legislation was now treated as a specialised function needing its own machinery.
- It **introduced open competition** for the civil services, throwing the
  covenanted service open to Indians. The **Macaulay Committee** was appointed in
  **1854** to work out the details.
- It extended Company rule **without specifying a time limit** — a clear signal
  that Parliament could end it whenever it chose.
- It introduced **local representation**: four of the six new legislative members
  were appointed by the provincial governments of **Madras, Bombay, Bengal and
  Agra**.

> [!exam] The single most-tested contrast in this chapter: **1833 attempted**
> open competition and it was killed; **1853 actually introduced it**, followed by
> the **Macaulay Committee in 1854**.

## The Crown Rule (1858–1947)

### Government of India Act, 1858 — the Company is abolished

Passed after the **Revolt of 1857**, and titled the *Act for the Good Government
of India*. It abolished the East India Company and transferred its powers,
territories and revenues to the **British Crown**.

- India would be governed **in the name of Her Majesty**. The Governor-General of
  India was redesignated **Viceroy**, the Crown's direct representative.
  **Lord Canning** became the first Viceroy.
- **Double government ended**: both the Board of Control and the Court of
  Directors were abolished.
- A new office of **Secretary of State for India** was created, holding complete
  authority over Indian administration. He sat in the **British Cabinet** and was
  answerable to the British Parliament.
- A **15-member Council of India** was created to assist him, with the Secretary
  of State as its chairman. The Secretary of State-in-Council was made a **body
  corporate**, able to sue and be sued.

> [!warn] The most common misreading of 1858: it changed **who** governed India,
> not **how**. It altered in no substantial way the system of government in India,
> confining itself to improving the machinery of control **in England**.

### Indian Councils Act, 1861 — Indians enter law-making

After 1857 the British concluded they needed Indian cooperation. Three Acts —
1861, 1892 and 1909 — follow from that policy.

- It began **representative institutions** by having the Viceroy nominate Indians
  as non-official members of his expanded council. In **1862** Canning nominated
  three: the **Raja of Benaras**, the **Maharaja of Patiala** and **Sir Dinkar
  Rao**.
- It began **decentralisation** by restoring legislative powers to Bombay and
  Madras — reversing the centralising drive that ran from 1773 to its peak in
  1833. This devolution eventually produced near-complete provincial autonomy in
  1937.
- New legislative councils were provided for **Bengal, the North-Western
  Provinces and Punjab** (established 1862, 1886 and 1897).
- It recognised the **portfolio system** introduced by Canning in **1859**, under
  which a council member took charge of a department and issued orders for it.
- It empowered the Viceroy to issue **ordinances** during an emergency without the
  council's concurrence, valid for **six months**.

### Indian Councils Act, 1892

- The number of non-official members in central and provincial councils rose, but
  the **official majority was retained**.
- Councils gained the power to **discuss the budget** and to **address questions**
  to the executive.
- Nominations were made on the recommendation of provincial councils, district
  boards, municipalities, universities, trade associations, zamindars and chambers
  of commerce.

> [!exam] This Act introduced **election indirectly** — but the **word "election"
> was never used**. The process was described as nomination on the recommendation
> of certain bodies. Examiners test that evasion directly.

### Indian Councils Act, 1909 — Morley–Minto Reforms

Named for **Lord Morley** (Secretary of State) and **Lord Minto** (Viceroy).

- Council sizes rose considerably; the Central Legislative Council went from
  **16 to 60** members.
- The **official majority was retained at the Centre**, but provincial councils
  were allowed a **non-official majority**.
- Members could ask **supplementary questions** and move **resolutions on the
  budget**.
- For the first time, Indians were associated with the **executive councils** of
  the Viceroy and Governors. **Satyendra Prasad Sinha** became the first Indian on
  the Viceroy's Executive Council, as **Law Member**.
- It introduced **communal representation** for Muslims through a **separate
  electorate** — Muslim members elected only by Muslim voters. The Act thus
  "legalised communalism", and **Lord Minto** is called the **Father of Communal
  Electorate**.

:::cloze
The Act of {{1858}} redesignated the Governor-General as {{Viceroy}}, the first being {{Lord Canning|Canning}}, and created the office of {{Secretary of State for India}}.
The Morley–Minto reforms of {{1909}} raised the Central Legislative Council from 16 to {{60}} members and gave a separate electorate to {{Muslims}}.
:::

### Government of India Act, 1919 — Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms

In **August 1917** the British declared for the first time that their objective
was the gradual introduction of **responsible government** in India. This Act
followed, coming into force in **1921**. Named for **Montagu** (Secretary of
State) and **Lord Chelmsford** (Viceroy).

- Subjects were divided into **central** and **provincial** lists by the
  *Devolution Rules*, relaxing central control — though the structure stayed
  centralised and unitary.
- Provincial subjects were divided again into **transferred** and **reserved**:

| | Transferred | Reserved |
|---|---|---|
| **Run by** | Governor **with ministers** answerable to the legislature | Governor **and executive council**, answerable to no one in India |
| **Subjects** | education, public health, local self-government, agriculture | police, justice, prisons, land revenue, finance |

  This split is **dyarchy** (Greek *di-arche*, double rule), and it was **largely
  unsuccessful**. Anything involving force or money stayed reserved, so ministers
  had responsibility without power.
- It introduced **bicameralism** and **direct elections** for the first time. The
  legislative council was replaced by a **Council of State** (upper) and a
  **Legislative Assembly** (lower), most members directly elected.
- **Three of six** members of the Viceroy's Executive Council (besides the
  Commander-in-Chief) had to be Indian.
- Separate electorates were extended to **Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians
  and Europeans**.
- Franchise was granted on the basis of **property, tax or education**.
- It created a **High Commissioner for India in London**, provided for a **public
  service commission** (the Central Public Service Commission followed in
  **1926**), **separated provincial budgets** from the central budget, and
  required a **statutory commission after ten years**.
- It proposed a **Chamber of Princes** (*Narendra Mandal*), inaugurated in
  **1921** with **120 members** — princes of 108 states plus 12 representatives of
  others — headed by the Viceroy.

### Simon Commission, Communal Award and Poona Pact

The statutory commission promised for 1929 was appointed early, in **November
1927**, under **Sir John Simon**. All **seven** members were British — not one
Indian — so every Indian party **boycotted** it. Reporting in **1930**, it
recommended abolishing dyarchy, extending responsible government in the provinces,
a federation of British India and the princely states, and continuing communal
electorates. Three **Round Table Conferences** followed, producing a *White Paper*
that fed into the 1935 Act.

In **August 1932** Prime Minister **Ramsay MacDonald** announced the **Communal
Award**, continuing separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, Indian Christians,
Anglo-Indians and Europeans — and **extending them to the Depressed Classes
(Scheduled Castes)**. Gandhi, seeing this as splitting Hindu society permanently,
began a **fast unto death** in **Yerawada Jail, Poona**. The resulting agreement
with **Dr B. R. Ambedkar** — the **Poona Pact** — kept a **joint Hindu electorate**
but gave the Depressed Classes **reserved seats** within it. That compromise was
carried into the 1935 Act.

:::cloze
Dyarchy was introduced in the {{provinces}} by the Government of India Act of {{1919}}, which also brought {{bicameralism}} and {{direct elections}} to India for the first time.
The Poona Pact replaced separate electorates for the Depressed Classes with {{reserved seats}} inside a {{joint}} electorate.
:::

### Government of India Act, 1935 — the blueprint

The longest and most detailed statute of British India: **321 sections and 10
schedules**. Most of the Constitution's machinery starts here.

- It provided for an **All-India Federation** of provinces and princely states.
  The states' accession was voluntary, they refused, and the **federation never
  came into being**.
- Powers were divided into three lists: **Federal (59 items)**, **Provincial (54)**
  and **Concurrent (36)**, with **residuary powers to the Viceroy**.
- It **abolished dyarchy in the provinces** and introduced **provincial
  autonomy**: the Governor had to act on the advice of ministers responsible to
  the provincial legislature. This worked from **1937 until the 1939**
  resignations.
- It **proposed dyarchy at the Centre** — which **never came into operation**.
- **Bicameralism** was introduced in **six of eleven** provinces: Bengal, Bombay,
  Madras, Bihar, Assam and the United Provinces.
- It reserved seats for the **Depressed Classes** and extended special
  representation to **women** and **labour**.
- It **abolished the Council of India** (created in 1858), leaving the Secretary
  of State with advisors.
- Franchise was extended to about **14 per cent** of the population.
- It provided for the **Reserve Bank of India**, a **Federal Court** (established
  **1937**), and **Federal, Provincial and Joint Public Service Commissions**, a
  **Federal Railway Authority** and an **Auditor-General of India**.
- It **separated Burma** from India and created the provinces of **Orissa and
  Sind**.

> [!tip] Dyarchy and bicameralism are mirror images, and that mirror is the trap.
> Dyarchy: **provinces in 1919**, abolished there in 1935, proposed **at the
> Centre in 1935** but never operated. Bicameralism: **at the Centre in 1919**,
> in **six provinces in 1935**.

### Indian Independence Act, 1947

On **20 February 1947** Prime Minister **Clement Attlee** announced that British
rule would end by **30 June 1948**. The Muslim League's demand for partition
followed, and on **3 June 1947** Viceroy **Lord Mountbatten** put forward the
partition plan, accepted by both the Congress and the League. The Act gave it
immediate effect.

- British rule **ended on 15 August 1947**, creating two independent dominions,
  **India and Pakistan**, each with the right to secede from the Commonwealth.
- The office of **Viceroy was abolished**; each dominion got a **Governor-General**
  appointed on the advice of its own cabinet.
- The **Constituent Assemblies** of both dominions could frame any constitution
  and **repeal any Act of the British Parliament, including this one**.
- The office of **Secretary of State for India was abolished**, his functions
  passing to the Secretary of State for Commonwealth Affairs.
- **British paramountcy over the princely states lapsed**; they were free to join
  India, join Pakistan, or remain independent.
- Until new constitutions were framed, both dominions were to be governed under
  the **Government of India Act, 1935**, with permitted modifications.
- The Governor-General and provincial governors became **constitutional (nominal)
  heads**, bound by ministerial advice. The King lost his power to veto bills, and
  the title **Emperor of India** was dropped.

At midnight of **14–15 August 1947**, **Lord Mountbatten** became the first
Governor-General of the Dominion of India and swore in **Jawaharlal Nehru** as
its first Prime Minister. The Constituent Assembly formed in 1946 became the
**Parliament of the Dominion**.

:::cloze
The Government of India Act of 1935 ran to {{321}} sections and divided powers into lists of {{59}}, {{54}} and {{36}} items, with residuary powers going to the {{Viceroy|Governor-General}}.
The Indian Independence Act ended British rule on {{15 August 1947}} and declared the lapse of British {{paramountcy}} over the princely states.
:::

|---|---|
| 1600 | Charter from Elizabeth I |
| 1765 | Diwani — the Company becomes a ruler |

| 1833 | Governor-General **of India**; Company purely administrative |

:::recall In one line — why did dyarchy collapse under the 1919 Act while provincial autonomy under the 1935 Act at least functioned?
Dyarchy divided a single province between ministers answerable to the legislature and a Governor answerable to nobody in India, so responsibility was split and failure could never be pinned on anyone; **1935 removed the split and made the whole provincial government answerable to its legislature**, which is why it ran from 1937 until the 1939 resignations.
:::
