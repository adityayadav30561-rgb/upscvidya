# Preamble of the Constitution

The **American Constitution** was the first to open with a preamble, and many
countries — India among them — followed. A preamble is the introduction to a
constitution, and it carries the summary or essence of what follows. The jurist
**N.A. Palkhivala** called ours the **'identity card of the Constitution'**.

Our Preamble is not an original composition. It is the **Objectives Resolution**
— moved by **Nehru on 13 December 1946**, adopted **22 January 1947** — turned
into formal constitutional language. And it has been amended exactly **once**,
by the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**.

Read the chapter as three questions, because that is how it is examined:

1. **What does it say?** The text, its four ingredients, and the nine key words.
2. **What is it worth legally?** The Supreme Court has answered this twice, and
   changed its mind in between.
3. **Can it be changed?** The answer is yes, but not in every respect — and that
   limit is the most important idea in the chapter.

:::predict The 42nd Amendment added three words to the Preamble. Two joined the list describing what kind of state India is — but the third went somewhere else entirely. Which word, and where did it go?
**Integrity**. It was not added to the 'Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic' description at all — it went into the **fraternity clause**, which now assures "the dignity of the individual and the **unity and integrity** of the Nation". So the state-description gained **two** words, not three.
:::

## The text

> **We, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA**, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into
> a **SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC** and to secure to all its
> citizens:
>
> **JUSTICE**, Social, Economic and Political;
> **LIBERTY** of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship;
> **EQUALITY** of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all;
> **FRATERNITY** assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and
> integrity of the Nation;
>
> **IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY** this **twenty-sixth day of November, 1949**,
> do hereby **ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION**.

Four things sit inside that single sentence, and they are asked as a set of
**four ingredients**:

1. **Source of authority** — the Constitution derives its authority from **the
   people of India**. Not from the Crown, not from Parliament.
2. **Nature of the Indian State** — sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic,
   republican.
3. **Objectives** — **justice, liberty, equality and fraternity**.
4. **Date of adoption** — **26 November 1949**.

> [!warn] The date inside the Preamble is **26 November 1949**, the date of
> **adoption**. It is *not* 26 January 1950, the date of **commencement**. A
> question that quotes the Preamble and asks for "the date mentioned" wants 1949.

:::cloze
The Preamble is based on the {{Objectives Resolution}}, moved by {{Nehru|Jawaharlal Nehru}} on {{13 December 1946}}.
The four ingredients are the source of {{authority}}, the nature of the state, the {{objectives}}, and the date of adoption — {{26 November 1949}}.
{{N.A. Palkhivala|Palkhivala}} called the Preamble the identity card of the Constitution.
:::

## Sovereign

**Sovereign** means India is neither a **dependency** nor a **dominion** of any
other nation. No authority stands above it, and it is free to conduct its own
affairs, internal and external.

The word records a journey, and the journey is the examinable part:

**Dependency (colony) until 1947 → Dominion, 15 August 1947 to 26 January 1950 →
Sovereign Republic from 26 January 1950.**

Two facts about India's international commitments are set as traps, because both
*look* like limits on sovereignty and neither is:

- In **1949** India declared the continuation of her full membership of the
  **Commonwealth of Nations**, accepting the British Crown as **Head of the
  Commonwealth**. This is an **extra-constitutional declaration** and does not
  affect sovereignty. Nehru's defence: *"It is an agreement by free will, to be
  terminated by free will."*
- India joined the **United Nations Organisation in 1945**. Membership of the UNO
  is likewise no limitation on sovereignty.

Being sovereign carries a concrete consequence: India can **acquire foreign
territory** and can **cede** part of its own territory to a foreign state.

> [!exam] **Pakistan remained a British dominion until 1956**, six years longer
> than India. The pair 1950/1956 is a favourite contrast.

:::cloze
India's political status was that of a {{dominion}} from 15 August 1947 until {{26 January 1950}}, when it became a sovereign republic.
{{Pakistan}} continued as a British dominion until {{1956}}.
India joined the United Nations Organisation in {{1945}}, which is no limitation on its {{sovereignty}}.
:::

## Socialist

The word **socialist** was added by the **42nd Amendment, 1976** — but the
content was already there. The **Directive Principles of State Policy** carried a
socialist direction from the beginning, so what the amendment did was make
**explicit what was already implicit**. The Congress had itself resolved to build
a **'socialistic pattern of society'** at its **Avadi session in 1955**.

The distinction that matters is which *kind* of socialism:

| | Democratic socialism (India) | Communistic socialism |
|---|---|---|
| Economy | **Mixed** — public and private coexist | All means of production nationalised |
| Private property | Retained | Abolished |
| Also called | — | **State socialism** |

Two judicial descriptions are quoted directly in exams. In **G.B. Pant
University of Agriculture and Technology v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2000)** the
Supreme Court said democratic socialism aims **"to end poverty, ignorance,
disease and inequality of opportunity"**. In **Nakara v. Union of India (1982)**
it said Indian socialism is a **blend of Marxism and Gandhism, leaning heavily
towards Gandhian socialism**.

One qualification the syllabus insists on: the **New Economic Policy of 1991** —
liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation — has **diluted** the socialist
credentials of the Indian state.

:::cloze
The Congress resolved to establish a socialistic pattern of society at its {{Avadi}} session in {{1955}}.
Indian socialism is {{democratic socialism}}, resting on a {{mixed economy}}, not communistic or state socialism.
In {{Nakara}} v. Union of India (1982) the Court called Indian socialism a blend of Marxism and {{Gandhism}}.
:::

## Secular

**Secular** was also added by the **42nd Amendment, 1976**. But in **1974** —
two years *before* the word existed in the text — the Supreme Court had already
held that although the words 'secular state' were not expressly mentioned, the
Constitution-makers plainly intended one, and had included **Articles 25 to 28**
guaranteeing the fundamental right to freedom of religion.

India's is the **positive** concept of secularism: **all religions have the same
status and support from the state**, irrespective of their strength. The Western
model is negative or separationist — a wall between religion and the state.

States are classified three ways by their attitude to religion, and India's
position is only clear against the other two:

| Type of state | Attitude | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Atheistic** | Anti-religion; condemns all religions | — |
| **Theocratic** | Pro-religion; one official state religion | Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka |
| **Secular** | Neutral; no state religion | **India**, USA |

Two definitions are attributed. **H.R. Gokhale**, then Union Law Minister, said
the state "will not have anything to do, as a state, with any religion excepting
to treat every religion equally". **P.B. Gajendragadkar**, a former Chief Justice
of India, put it as: the state **"is not irreligious or anti-religious; it gives
equal freedom to all religions"**.

> [!exam] The sequence is the trap. The **guarantee (Articles 25–28) came first**
> and the **label ('secular', 1976) came second** — and the Court had said so in
> **1974**, before the word was inserted.

:::cloze
The word 'secular' was inserted by the {{42nd}} Amendment in {{1976}}, but the Supreme Court had already held in {{1974}} that the framers intended a secular state.
Freedom of religion is guaranteed by Articles {{25}} to {{28}}.
A state with an official state religion is a {{theocratic}} state; India is a {{secular}} one.
:::

## Democratic

**Democracy** comes from the Greek **demos** (people) and **kratia** (rule). A
democratic polity rests on **popular sovereignty** — supreme power held by the
people.

Democracy is **direct** or **indirect**. In a direct democracy the people
exercise power themselves, as in **Switzerland**, through four devices:

| Device | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Referendum** | A proposed law is referred to the electorate for their direct vote |
| **Initiative** | The people propose a bill to the legislature for enactment |
| **Recall** | Voters remove a representative or officer before his term expires |
| **Plebiscite** | Opinion is obtained on an issue of public importance, usually a territorial dispute |

In an **indirect** or **representative** democracy, elected representatives
exercise power. It comes in two kinds — **parliamentary** and **presidential** —
and India adopted **representative parliamentary democracy**, under which the
executive is responsible to the legislature for all its policies and actions.
Universal adult franchise, periodic elections, rule of law, independence of the
judiciary and the absence of discrimination are its manifestations.

The word carries more than politics. **Democratic** in the Preamble is used
broadly, covering **political, social and economic democracy**. Ambedkar pressed
exactly this in his concluding speech to the Constituent Assembly on
**25 November 1949**:

> Political democracy cannot last unless there lies at the base of it social
> democracy… **Liberty, equality and fraternity are not separate items in a
> trinity. They form a union of trinity** — to divorce one from the other is to
> defeat the very purpose of democracy. Without equality, liberty would produce
> the supremacy of the few over the many. Equality without liberty would kill
> individual initiative.

:::cloze
The four devices of direct democracy are {{referendum}}, {{initiative}}, {{recall}} and {{plebiscite}}.
{{Switzerland}} is the standard example of direct democracy, while India has representative {{parliamentary}} democracy.
Ambedkar told the Constituent Assembly on {{25 November 1949}} that liberty, equality and fraternity form a {{union of trinity}}.
:::

## Republic

A democratic polity is either a **monarchy** or a **republic**, and the test is
how the head of state gets the job:

- **Monarchy** — the head of state holds the office by **heredity**, through
  succession. Example: **Britain**.
- **Republic** — the head of state is **elected**, directly or indirectly, for a
  **fixed period**. Example: the **USA**.

India is a republic because its head, the **President**, is **indirectly
elected for a fixed term of five years**.

'Republic' carries two further meanings worth holding separately, because both
appear as options:

1. **Political sovereignty vests in the people**, not in a single individual
   such as a king.
2. **There is no privileged class** — every public office is open to every
   citizen without discrimination.

:::cloze
In a {{monarchy}} the head of state holds office by heredity, as in {{Britain}}; in a {{republic}} the head of state is elected for a fixed period.
India's President is elected {{indirectly}} for a term of {{5|five}} years.
:::

## Justice, liberty, equality, fraternity

The four objectives come from two revolutions, and swapping them is the single
most common error in this chapter.

> [!tip] **Justice** ← the **Russian Revolution, 1917**.
> **Liberty, equality and fraternity** ← the **French Revolution, 1789–1799**.

### Justice — three forms

- **Social justice** — equal treatment with no distinction of caste, colour,
  race, religion or sex; no privileges for any section; improvement of the
  backward classes (SCs, STs, OBCs) and women.
- **Economic justice** — no discrimination on economic grounds; elimination of
  glaring inequalities in wealth, income and property.
- **Political justice** — equal political rights, equal access to office, equal
  voice in government.

Social justice and economic justice taken together are called **distributive
justice**.

### Liberty — qualified, not absolute

Liberty means the absence of restraints on individual activity together with
opportunities for the development of personality. The Preamble secures liberty of
**thought, expression, belief, faith and worship**, delivered through the
Fundamental Rights and enforceable in court.

But **liberty is not licence**. It is enjoyed within the limitations written into
the Constitution itself — so the liberty of the Preamble, like the Fundamental
Rights, is **not absolute but qualified**.

### Equality — three dimensions

| Dimension | Provisions |
|---|---|
| **Civic** | **Article 14** equality before law · **15** no discrimination · **16** equal opportunity in public employment · **17** abolition of untouchability · **18** abolition of titles |
| **Political** | **Article 325** no ineligibility for electoral rolls on grounds of religion, race, caste or sex · **326** adult suffrage |
| **Economic** | **Article 39** (a Directive Principle) equal means of livelihood, equal pay for equal work |

### Fraternity — dignity and unity

Fraternity means a **sense of brotherhood**. The Constitution promotes it through
**single citizenship**, and through the Fundamental Duty in **Article 51-A** to
promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood transcending religious,
linguistic, regional and sectional diversities.

The Preamble says fraternity must assure **two** things:

1. **The dignity of the individual.** **K.M. Munshi** of the Drafting Committee
   explained that this means the Constitution recognises that **the personality
   of every individual is sacred** — not merely that material betterment and a
   democratic set-up are secured.
2. **The unity and integrity of the Nation.** The word **integrity** was added by
   the **42nd Amendment, 1976**. This covers both the **psychological and the
   territorial** dimensions of national integration, and aims at overcoming
   communalism, regionalism, casteism, linguism and secessionism. **Article 1**
   calls India a **'Union of States'** precisely to make clear that no state may
   secede.

:::cloze
The ideal of justice came from the {{Russian Revolution}} of {{1917}}, and liberty, equality and fraternity from the {{French Revolution}}.
Social justice plus economic justice together make {{distributive justice}}.
Political equality rests on Articles {{325}} and {{326}}, and economic equality on Article {{39}}.
:::

## What the Preamble is worth — the tributes

The Preamble carries the basic philosophy and the fundamental political, moral
and religious values on which the Constitution rests. Six characterisations are
asked directly, and the marks are in the name-to-phrase pairing:

| Who | What they called it |
|---|---|
| **N.A. Palkhivala** | The **'identity card of the Constitution'** |
| **K.M. Munshi** | The **'horoscope of our sovereign democratic republic'** |
| **Sir Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar** | *"expresses what we had thought or dreamt so long"* |
| **Pandit Thakur Das Bhargava** | The **'soul of the Constitution'**, its **'key'**, a **'jewel set in the Constitution'** |
| **Sir Ernest Barker** | The **'key-note'** to the Constitution |
| **M. Hidayatullah** | Resembles the **US Declaration of Independence** but is more — *"a solemn resolve, which nothing but a revolution can alter"* |

Barker, an English political scientist, was so moved by the text that he quoted
it at the opening of his book **Principles of Social and Political Theory
(1951)**. Hidayatullah was a **former Chief Justice of India**.

:::cloze
{{K.M. Munshi|Munshi}} called the Preamble the horoscope of our sovereign democratic republic, while {{Ernest Barker|Sir Ernest Barker|Barker}} called it the key-note to the Constitution.
{{Thakur Das Bhargava|Pandit Thakur Das Bhargava|Bhargava}} called it the soul of the Constitution and a jewel set in it.
:::

## Is the Preamble part of the Constitution?

This is the chapter's real controversy, and the Supreme Court has answered it
twice — in opposite directions.

**Berubari Union (1960)** was a **Presidential reference under Article 143**,
arising from the Indo-Pakistan agreement of 1958 on the Berubari union and the
exchange of enclaves. The Court said the Preamble shows the **general purposes**
behind the Constitution's provisions and is a **key to the minds of the makers**,
so where an Article's terms are ambiguous or bear more than one meaning, the
Preamble may assist interpretation. Having said all that, it then held that the
**Preamble is not a part of the Constitution**.

**Kesavananda Bharati (1973)** rejected that opinion and held that the
**Preamble is a part of the Constitution** — that it is of extreme importance and
the Constitution should be read and interpreted in the light of the grand and
noble vision expressed in it. **LIC of India (1995)** held the same again.

The current position is also the historically accurate one. The Preamble was
enacted by the Constituent Assembly like any other part — though **after** the
rest of the Constitution, so that it would conform to what had already been
adopted. When the President of the Assembly put it to the vote, the motion he
read out was: **"The question is that Preamble stands part of the Constitution."**

Two limits survive all of this, and both are examined:

1. The Preamble is **neither a source of power** to the legislature **nor a
   prohibition** upon its powers.
2. It is **non-justiciable** — its provisions are **not enforceable in a court of
   law**.

> [!warn] Do not collapse the two ideas. The Preamble **is part of** the
> Constitution (Kesavananda) and is **still non-justiciable**. Being part of the
> document and being independently enforceable are different things.

:::cloze
In {{Berubari Union|Berubari}} (1960) the Supreme Court held the Preamble was {{not}} a part of the Constitution.
That view was reversed in {{Kesavananda Bharati}} (1973) and reaffirmed in the {{LIC of India}} case (1995).
The Preamble is {{non-justiciable}} and is neither a source of power nor a prohibition upon the {{legislature}}.
:::

## Can the Preamble be amended?

The question first arose in **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**. The petitioner argued
that the Preamble could not be amended at all, since it was not part of the
Constitution — and, in the alternative, that the amending power in **Article
368** could not be used to destroy or damage the **basic elements** enshrined in
it.

The Court's answer had two halves. First, the Preamble **is** part of the
Constitution, and its own contrary opinion in **Berubari Union (1960) was
wrong**. Second, the Preamble **can therefore be amended under Article 368** —
but **not so as to alter the 'basic features'**. In the Court's words:

> The edifice of our Constitution is based upon the basic elements mentioned in
> the Preamble. If any of these elements are removed, the structure will not
> survive and it will not be the same Constitution, or it cannot maintain its
> identity.

In practice the power has been used **once**. The **42nd Constitutional Amendment
Act, 1976** added **Socialist**, **Secular** and **Integrity**, and that
amendment **was held valid** — it added to the basic elements rather than
removing any.

:::cloze
The amendability of the Preamble was decided in {{Kesavananda Bharati}} (1973): it can be amended under Article {{368}} but not so as to alter the {{basic features|basic structure}}.
The Preamble has been amended {{once|1}} so far, by the {{42nd}} Amendment Act of {{1976}}.
:::

:::recall In one line — the Preamble is non-justiciable and grants no power to anybody. Why, then, did the Supreme Court treat the question of amending it as constitutionally serious?
Because it is **part of the Constitution and carries the basic elements on which the whole edifice rests** — so an amendment that removed one of those elements would not merely change a preface, it would produce a document that could no longer maintain its identity as the same Constitution. Non-justiciability limits how the Preamble can be **used in court**; it says nothing about its **structural importance**.
:::
