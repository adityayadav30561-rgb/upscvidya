# Salient Features of the Constitution

Chapter 3 gave you the vocabulary — written, rigid, federal, unitary. This
chapter spends that vocabulary on one document and finds that **India refuses
every clean label**. The Constitution is written but not exhaustive, rigid but
amendable by simple majority in parts, federal in structure but unitary the
moment an emergency is proclaimed.

Two warnings before the list. First, **the Constitution of 1949 is not the
Constitution of today.** Amendments have changed the features substantially —
the **7th, 42nd, 44th, 73rd, 74th, 97th and 101st** especially. The **42nd
Amendment Act, 1976** made so many changes at once that it is called the **"Mini
Constitution"**. Every "originally … presently" pair below is examinable as a
pair, never as a single number.

Second, that power to amend is not unlimited. In **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**
the Supreme Court held that Parliament's constituent power under **Article 368**
cannot be used to alter the **'basic structure'** of the Constitution. So the
features below are not merely descriptive — some of them are beyond the reach of
the amending body itself.

:::predict One of the seventeen features listed below was removed from the Constitution altogether by a later amendment, and survives today only in a weaker form elsewhere in the document. Which one, and where did it go?
The **Right to Property**. It was a Fundamental Right under **Article 31** until the **44th Amendment Act, 1978** deleted it; it now survives as an ordinary **legal right under Article 300-A** in **Part XII**, enforceable through the courts but no longer through Article 32.
:::

## 1. The lengthiest written constitution in the world

Constitutions are written or unwritten — the USA has the classic written one,
the UK the classic unwritten one. India's is **written, and the longest in the
world**.

Learn the two columns, never one alone:

| | Originally (1949) | Presently |
|---|---|---|
| Articles | **395** | about **470** |
| Parts | **22** | **25** |
| Schedules | **8** | **12** |

Since 1951, roughly **20 Articles and one Part (VII)** have been **deleted**,
while about **95 Articles, four Parts (IV-A, IX-A, IX-B and XIV-A) and four
Schedules (9th, 10th, 11th and 12th)** have been **added**.

Four reasons are given for the bulk:

1. **Geographical vastness and diversity** of the country.
2. The **historical influence of the Government of India Act, 1935**, itself a
   bulky statute.
3. **A single constitution for both the Centre and the states**, where a country
   like the USA leaves each state to write its own.
4. The **dominance of legal experts** in the Constituent Assembly.

The document therefore carries not only fundamental principles of governance but
also **detailed administrative provisions** that other countries leave to
ordinary legislation or to convention.

> [!note] **Jammu and Kashmir** was the one exception to "a single constitution":
> it had its **own constitution** under **Article 370** until 2019. The
> **Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order, 2019** superseded the
> 1954 Order and extended the whole Constitution to the state, and the **Jammu
> and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019** split it into two union territories —
> **Jammu & Kashmir** and **Ladakh**.

:::cloze
Originally the Constitution had {{395}} Articles in {{22}} Parts and {{8|eight}} Schedules; today it has about {{470}} Articles in {{25}} Parts and {{12|twelve}} Schedules.
Jammu and Kashmir had its own constitution under Article {{370}} until {{2019}}.
:::

## 2. Drawn from various sources

Ambedkar said the Constitution was framed after **"ransacking all the known
Constitutions of the World."** The borrowings fall into three layers:

- The **structural part** comes mainly from the **Government of India Act,
  1935** — the federal scheme, the judiciary, the Governors, the emergency
  powers and the public service commissions. **More than half** the provisions
  of the Constitution resemble that Act, which is why it is called the most
  profound single source.
- The **philosophical part** is split: **Fundamental Rights from the American**
  Constitution, **Directive Principles from the Irish** Constitution.
- The **political part** — cabinet government and the relations between the
  executive and the legislature — comes from the **British** Constitution.

Other features come from **Canada, Australia, Germany (Weimar), the USSR, France,
South Africa and Japan**. The full breakdown is in the sources table at the end
of this chapter, and it is worth memorising line by line — it is one of the most
heavily asked tables in all of Polity.

:::cloze
Ambedkar said the Constitution was framed after {{ransacking}} all the known Constitutions of the World.
Fundamental Rights were drawn from the {{American|US|USA}} Constitution and the Directive Principles from the {{Irish|Ireland}} Constitution, while the structural part came mainly from the {{Government of India Act, 1935|Government of India Act 1935}}.
:::

## 3. A blend of rigidity and flexibility

**Article 368** provides two amendment routes:

1. By a **special majority of Parliament** — two-thirds of the members of each
   House present and voting, *plus* a majority of the total membership of that
   House.
2. By a **special majority plus ratification by half the states**.

And there is a third route outside Article 368 altogether: some provisions can be
amended by a **simple majority** of Parliament, in the ordinary legislative
process, and such changes are **not deemed amendments** of the Constitution for
the purposes of Article 368.

The verdict from Chapter 3 stands: the Constitution is **neither rigid nor
flexible, but a synthesis of both**.

## 4. A federal system with a unitary bias

The Constitution establishes a federal system — and then loads it towards the
Centre. Both lists are asked, so learn them as two columns:

| Federal features | Unitary features |
|---|---|
| Two governments | A strong Centre |
| Division of powers | A single constitution |
| Written constitution | Single citizenship |
| Supremacy of the constitution | Flexibility of the constitution |
| Rigidity | Integrated judiciary |
| Independent judiciary | Centre-appointed Governors |
| Bicameralism | All-India Services and emergency provisions |

The giveaway is in the text itself: the word **'Federation' is never used**.
**Article 1** describes India as a **'Union of States'**, and the phrase was
chosen to convey two things — that the Indian federation is **not the result of
an agreement among the states**, and that **no state has the right to secede**.

Because of that tilt, the Constitution has attracted a set of labels, each
belonging to a particular writer:

| Description | Author |
|---|---|
| **Quasi-federal** | **K.C. Wheare** |
| **Bargaining federalism** | **Morris-Jones** |
| **Co-operative federalism** | **Granville Austin** |
| **Federation with a centralising tendency** | **Ivor Jennings** |

It is also described generally as **'federal in form but unitary in spirit'**.

:::cloze
The word 'Federation' is nowhere used in the Constitution; Article {{1}} describes India as a {{Union of States}}.
The phrase conveys that the federation is not the result of an {{agreement}} among the states and that no state has the right to {{secede}}.
:::

:::cloze
{{Morris-Jones|Morris Jones}} called Indian federalism bargaining federalism, while {{Granville Austin|Austin}} called it co-operative federalism.
{{Ivor Jennings|Jennings}} described India as a federation with a {{centralising}} tendency.
:::

## 5. A parliamentary form of government

The framers chose the **British** model — variously called the **Westminster**,
**responsible** or **cabinet** form — over the **American presidential** model.
The difference is fundamental: the parliamentary system rests on **co-operation
between the legislature and the executive**, the presidential system on their
**separation**. The parliamentary system was adopted **both at the Centre and in
the states**.

Its features:

1. A **nominal executive** (President or Governor) alongside a **real executive**
   (the Council of Ministers).
2. **Majority party rule.**
3. **Collective responsibility** of the executive to the legislature.
4. **Ministers are members of the legislature.**
5. **Leadership of the Prime Minister or Chief Minister.**
6. **Dissolution of the lower House** is possible.

Two differences from Britain are examined constantly. The **Indian Parliament is
not sovereign** — it is limited by a written constitution, federalism, fundamental
rights and judicial review, whereas the British Parliament is sovereign. And
India is a **republic** with an elected head of state, while Britain is a
**monarchy** with a hereditary one.

Because the Prime Minister's role has become so central, scholars now describe
the Indian system as **'Prime Ministerial Government'**.

> [!exam] The pairing to hold: **parliamentary system = co-operation**,
> **presidential system = separation of powers**. The word 'separation' in an
> option about the parliamentary system is almost always wrong — except in
> **Article 50**, which separates the judiciary from the executive.

## 6. A synthesis of parliamentary sovereignty and judicial supremacy

Two principles pull against each other. **Parliamentary sovereignty** is the
British doctrine; **judicial supremacy** is the American one. India took a middle
path.

The Supreme Court can strike down parliamentary laws through **judicial review**,
but Parliament can amend most of the Constitution through its **constituent
power**. Neither is absolute.

Crucially, India's judicial review is **narrower** than America's, and the reason
is textual: **Article 21** guarantees liberty against deprivation except by
**'procedure established by law'** — the Japanese formulation — where the US
Constitution uses **'due process of law'**, which lets American courts test the
*reasonableness* of a law and not merely its existence.

:::cloze
India's judicial review is narrower than that of the United States because Article {{21}} uses the expression {{procedure established by law}}, taken from the {{Japanese}} Constitution.
The American Constitution instead uses {{due process of law}}.
:::

## 7. An integrated and independent judiciary

India has a **single integrated judicial system** — the **Supreme Court** at the
top, **High Courts** below it, and **district and subordinate courts** below
them. The same set of courts enforces **both central and state laws**, unlike
the USA where federal and state judiciaries run in parallel.

The Supreme Court wears four hats: **federal court**, **highest court of
appeal**, **guarantor of the Fundamental Rights**, and **guardian of the
Constitution**.

Its independence is secured by a specific list of devices:

- **Security of tenure** and **service conditions that cannot be varied to a
  judge's disadvantage**
- **Expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**
- **No discussion of a judge's conduct in the legislature**
- **A ban on practice after retirement**
- **Power to punish for its contempt**
- **Separation of the judiciary from the executive**

## 8. Fundamental Rights (Part III)

| Right | Articles |
|---|---|
| Right to Equality | **14–18** |
| Right to Freedom | **19–22** |
| Right against Exploitation | **23–24** |
| Right to Freedom of Religion | **25–28** |
| Cultural and Educational Rights | **29–30** |
| Right to Constitutional Remedies | **32** |

Originally there were **seven** Fundamental Rights. The **Right to Property
(Article 31)** was **deleted by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978** and made an
ordinary **legal right under Article 300-A** in **Part XII** — which is why the
list above has six.

Fundamental Rights are **justiciable**: the Supreme Court can be moved directly
under Article 32 for **five writs** — **habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition,
certiorari and quo warranto**.

They are not absolute. They carry **reasonable restrictions**, Parliament can
amend them, and they can be **suspended during a National Emergency** — with one
protected pair: **Articles 20 and 21 can never be suspended**.

> [!warn] Two numbers get confused: **7 rights originally, 6 today** (property
> deleted), and **5 writs**. And the exception to suspension is **Articles 20 and
> 21**, not Article 21 alone.

:::cloze
Originally there were {{7|seven}} Fundamental Rights; the Right to Property was deleted by the {{44th}} Amendment Act of {{1978}} and made a legal right under Article {{300-A|300A}}.
During a National Emergency, Fundamental Rights may be suspended except those under Articles {{20}} and {{21}}.
:::

## 9. Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV)

Ambedkar called the Directive Principles a **'novel feature'** of the
Constitution. They fall into three groups — **socialistic, Gandhian and
liberal-intellectual** — and their object is **social and economic democracy**,
that is, a **welfare state**.

They are **non-justiciable**: no court can enforce them. But the Constitution
declares them **"fundamental in the governance of the country"**, and their real
sanction is **political** — public opinion at the next election.

In **Minerva Mills (1980)** the Supreme Court held that the Constitution is
founded on the **balance** between the Fundamental Rights and the Directive
Principles, so neither may be given absolute primacy over the other.

## 10. Fundamental Duties (Part IV-A, Article 51-A)

Fundamental Duties were **not in the original Constitution**. They were added by
the **42nd Amendment Act, 1976**, during the Emergency of 1975–77, on the
recommendation of the **Swaran Singh Committee**. Ten duties were inserted; the
**86th Amendment Act, 2002** added one more, bringing the total to **eleven**.

They require citizens to respect the Constitution, the National Flag and the
National Anthem, to protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India, to
promote the spirit of common brotherhood, to preserve the composite culture, and
so on. Like the Directive Principles, they are **non-justiciable**.

:::cloze
Fundamental Duties were added by the {{42nd}} Amendment Act of {{1976}} on the recommendation of the {{Swaran Singh}} Committee, and are contained in Part {{IV-A|IVA}}, Article {{51-A|51A}}.
The {{86th}} Amendment Act of {{2002}} raised their number to {{11|eleven}}.
:::

## 11. A secular state

The word **'secular'** was **not in the original Preamble** — it was added by the
**42nd Amendment Act, 1976**. But the substance was there from the start, spread
across a set of provisions:

- **Article 14** — equality before the law
- **Article 15** — no discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or
  place of birth
- **Article 16** — equality of opportunity in public employment
- **Article 25** — freedom of conscience and free profession, practice and
  propagation of religion
- **Article 26** — freedom of religious denominations to manage their own affairs
- **Article 27** — no compulsion to pay taxes for promoting any religion
- **Article 28** — no religious instruction in wholly state-funded institutions
- **Articles 29 and 30** — protection of distinct culture, and the right of
  minorities to establish and administer educational institutions
- **Article 44** — the Uniform Civil Code as a directive goal

India's is described as **positive secularism** — equal respect for all religions
— as against the Western **negative or separationist** model, which builds a wall
between religion and the state. The Constitution abolished **communal
representation** but retained **temporary reservation of seats for Scheduled
Castes and Scheduled Tribes**.

## 12. Universal adult franchise

Every citizen aged **18 or above** may vote, without any qualification of
property, income, education or sex. The age was originally **21**, and was
reduced to 18 by the **61st Amendment Act, 1988**, with effect from **1989**.

Given the country's size, population, poverty and illiteracy at the time, this
was a bold experiment — no comparable democracy had begun with full adult
suffrage.

## 13. Single citizenship

India has a **dual polity** but only **single citizenship**. A citizen is a
citizen of India alone, never of a state — unlike the USA, where a person holds
both national and state citizenship. Every citizen carries the **same political
and civil rights everywhere in the country**, irrespective of the state of birth
or residence.

> [!tip] Federal *structure*, unitary *citizenship*. It is one of the clearest
> illustrations of the unitary bias in feature 4, and it comes from the
> **British** Constitution.

:::cloze
The voting age was reduced from {{21}} to {{18}} by the {{61st}} Amendment Act of {{1988}}.
Although India has a dual polity, the Constitution provides for {{single}} citizenship, unlike the {{USA|United States|American}} model of dual citizenship.
:::

## 14. Independent bodies

The Constitution creates certain bodies and then insulates them from both the
executive and the legislature:

1. **Election Commission** — free and fair elections to Parliament, the state
   legislatures, and the offices of **President and Vice-President**.
2. **Comptroller and Auditor-General of India** — audits the accounts of the
   Centre and the states; the **guardian of the public purse**.
3. **Union Public Service Commission** — recruitment to the All-India Services
   and the higher Central services; advises the President on disciplinary
   matters.
4. **State Public Service Commissions** — the same functions for the states;
   advise the Governor.

Their independence rests on the same three devices as the judiciary's: **security
of tenure**, **service conditions that cannot be varied to their disadvantage**,
and **expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**.

## 15. Emergency provisions

| Type | Ground | Article |
|---|---|---|
| **National Emergency** | War, external aggression or armed rebellion | **352** |
| **President's Rule** | Failure of constitutional machinery in a state | **356** |
| **President's Rule** | Failure to comply with Union directions | **365** |
| **Financial Emergency** | Threat to the financial stability or credit of India | **360** |

The purpose is to enable the Centre to meet an abnormal situation, and the effect
is dramatic: during an emergency the **federal structure converts into a unitary
one without any formal amendment** of the Constitution. That automatic
transformation is described as a **unique feature** of the Indian Constitution.

:::cloze
A National Emergency is proclaimed under Article {{352}}, President's Rule under Article {{356}} and a Financial Emergency under Article {{360}}.
During an emergency the federal structure converts into a {{unitary}} one without any formal {{amendment}} of the Constitution.
:::

## 16. Three-tier government

The original Constitution had two tiers — Centre and states. Two amendments of
**1992** added a third:

- The **73rd Amendment Act, 1992** gave constitutional status to the
  **Panchayats**, adding **Part IX** and the **Eleventh Schedule**, with a
  three-tier structure at village, intermediate and district levels.
- The **74th Amendment Act, 1992** gave constitutional status to the
  **Municipalities**, adding **Part IX-A** and the **Twelfth Schedule**, with
  three types of urban body — **Nagar Panchayat, Municipal Council and Municipal
  Corporation**.

A tier of local government written into the constitution itself is **not found in
other constitutions of the world**.

## 17. Co-operative societies

The **97th Amendment Act, 2011** gave co-operatives constitutional status through
**three** changes:

1. Made the **right to form co-operative societies a Fundamental Right** under
   **Article 19**.
2. Added a **new Directive Principle** on the promotion of co-operative
   societies — **Article 43-B**.
3. Added a **new Part IX-B**, "The Co-operative Societies", covering
   **Articles 243-ZH to 243-ZT**.

:::cloze
The {{73rd}} Amendment Act of 1992 added Part {{IX}} and the {{Eleventh|11th}} Schedule for the Panchayats, while the {{74th}} Amendment added Part {{IX-A|IXA}} and the {{Twelfth|12th}} Schedule for the Municipalities.
The {{97th}} Amendment Act of {{2011}} added Part IX-B and the new Directive Principle in Article {{43-B|43B}}.
:::

## What the critics say

Six charges, each attached to named critics. Learn the name with the phrase — the
phrases are what appear in options.

1. **A borrowed constitution.** Called a **'bag of borrowings'** and a
   **'patchwork'**. Ambedkar's answer: at this late stage in the history of
   constitution-making, all constitutions look alike in their fundamentals, so
   the only thing that can be new is the **variations** — introduced to remove
   faults and suit the country's needs.
2. **A carbon copy of the 1935 Act.** **N. Srinivasan** and **Ivor Jennings**
   called it a near-textual reproduction; **P.R. Deshmukh** said it was "the
   Government of India Act, 1935, with **adult franchise** added". Ambedkar
   replied that there is no shame in borrowing — **"nobody holds patent
   rights"** over the fundamental ideas of a constitution — and regretted only
   that what was borrowed was mostly administrative detail.
3. **Un-Indian or anti-Indian.** **K. Hanumanthaiya**, **Lokanath Misra** and
   **Lakshminarayan Sahu** attacked it as foreign to the Indian spirit —
   Hanumanthaiya's line was that the Assembly wanted the music of the **veena or
   the sitar** and got instead the **music of an English band**.
4. **Un-Gandhian.** It does not build the polity upward from **village
   panchayats**, as Gandhian philosophy required. **K. Hanumanthaiya** and
   **T. Prakasam** pressed this charge, connecting it to Ambedkar's distance
   from the Gandhian movement.
5. **Elephantine size.** Too bulky and detailed. **Ivor Jennings** called the
   borrowings poorly selected and the document too long and complicated;
   **H.V. Kamath** joked that the Assembly's emblem — the **elephant** — was
   fitting.
6. **A paradise for lawyers.** Too legalistic and complex. **Ivor Jennings**
   called it a **"lawyer's paradise"**; **H.K. Maheswari** and **P.R. Deshmukh**
   objected that a document meant to be **"vibrating and pulsating"** had been
   drowned in verbiage that would encourage litigation.

> [!exam] **Ivor Jennings appears three times** — carbon copy, elephantine size
> and lawyer's paradise. **K. Hanumanthaiya** appears twice — un-Indian and
> un-Gandhian. If a criticism question names a Briton, Jennings is the safe
> guess; if it quotes an Indian musical instrument, it is Hanumanthaiya.

:::cloze
{{Ivor Jennings|Jennings}} called the Constitution a lawyer's {{paradise}}.
{{P.R. Deshmukh|Deshmukh}} described it as the Government of India Act, 1935, with {{adult franchise}} added.
{{K. Hanumanthaiya|Hanumanthaiya}} complained that the Assembly wanted the music of the veena or sitar but got the music of an {{English band}}.
:::

### Sources of the Constitution

| Source | Features borrowed |
|---|---|
| **Government of India Act, 1935** | Federal scheme, office of Governor, judiciary, Public Service Commissions, emergency provisions, administrative details |
| **British Constitution** | Parliamentary government, rule of law, legislative procedure, single citizenship, cabinet system, prerogative writs, parliamentary privileges, bicameralism |
| **US Constitution** | Fundamental Rights, independence of the judiciary, judicial review, impeachment of the President, removal of Supreme Court and High Court judges, post of Vice-President |
| **Irish Constitution** | Directive Principles, nomination of Rajya Sabha members, method of election of the President |
| **Canadian Constitution** | Federation with a strong Centre, residuary powers with the Centre, appointment of Governors by the Centre |
| **Australian Constitution** | Concurrent List, freedom of trade and commerce, joint sitting of the two Houses |
| **Weimar Constitution (Germany)** | Suspension of Fundamental Rights during an emergency |
| **Soviet Constitution (USSR)** | Fundamental Duties, the ideal of justice in the Preamble |
| **French Constitution** | Republic, and the ideals of liberty, equality and fraternity |
| **South African Constitution** | Amendment procedure, election of Rajya Sabha members |
| **Japanese Constitution** | 'Procedure established by law' |

> [!tip] The two hardest rows are **Ireland** and **South Africa**, because both
> touch the Rajya Sabha. Ireland gave the **nomination** of members; South Africa
> gave their **election**. Ireland also gave the **manner of electing the
> President**, South Africa the **amendment procedure**.

:::cloze
The {{Canadian|Canada}} Constitution gave India a federation with a strong Centre and residuary powers with the Centre, while the {{Australian|Australia}} Constitution gave the Concurrent List and the joint sitting of the two Houses.
The {{Weimar|German|Germany}} Constitution gave the suspension of Fundamental Rights during an emergency, and the {{Soviet|USSR|Russian}} Constitution the Fundamental Duties.
:::

:::recall In one line — the Constitution is called federal, yet an emergency turns it unitary without a single word of it being amended. How is that possible?
Because the **unitary provisions are already inside the federal document** — the emergency provisions in Part XVIII are themselves part of the Constitution, so their operation is a change of working, not of text. This is why India is called **quasi-federal** and **'federal in form but unitary in spirit'**, and why the automatic transformation is treated as a unique feature.
:::
