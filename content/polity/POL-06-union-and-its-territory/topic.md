# Union and Its Territory

**Articles 1 to 4**, in **Part I**, settle what India *is* as a territorial
entity — its name, the kind of polity it forms, what counts as its territory,
and who is allowed to redraw the map. Four short Articles, but they carry one
of the most heavily examined ideas in Polity: **states in India have no
guaranteed existence**.

Read the chapter as two halves. The first half is the **law** — what Articles 1
to 4 permit. The second half is the **history** — how a map of 552 princely
states and four classes of provinces became today's **28 states and 8 union
territories**.

:::predict Parliament can wipe a state off the map without asking it. So can Parliament hand a piece of Indian land to a foreign country the same way?
**No.** The Supreme Court held in the **Berubari Union case (1960)** that the Article 3 power to *diminish the area of a state* does **not** extend to **ceding territory to a foreign country**. That needs a **constitutional amendment under Article 368** — which is exactly why the **9th Amendment Act, 1960** had to be passed to transfer Berubari to Pakistan.
:::

## Article 1 — the name and the polity

Article 1 declares **"India, that is, Bharat, shall be a Union of States."**
Two decisions are packed into that line.

**The name.** The Constituent Assembly never reached agreement. One group
wanted the traditional **Bharat**, another the modern **India**, so the Assembly
adopted a **mix of both**.

**The polity.** The Constitution is **federal in structure** but the country is
called a **Union**. **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** gave two reasons for preferring
**'Union of States'** over **'Federation of States'**:

1. The Indian federation is **not the result of an agreement** among the states,
   unlike the American federation.
2. The states have **no right to secede** from the federation.

The federation is a Union because it is **indestructible**. The country is an
integral whole, divided into states only for the **convenience of
administration**.

> [!tip] Two reasons, one consequence: no agreement + no secession = indestructible Union.

:::cloze
Article 1 describes India as a {{Union of States}}, not a Federation of States.
Ambedkar's two reasons: the federation is not the result of an {{agreement}} among states, and states have no right to {{secede}}.
:::

## What counts as the territory of India

Article 1 sorts the territory of India into **three categories**:

1. **Territories of the states**
2. **Union territories**
3. **Territories that may be acquired** by the Government of India at any time

The names of the states and union territories, with their territorial extent,
are listed in the **First Schedule**.

The Constitution's provisions apply to all states **in the same manner**. But
the **special provisions under Part XXI** — for Maharashtra, Gujarat, Nagaland,
Assam, Manipur, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Sikkim, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh,
Goa and Karnataka — **override** the general provisions relating to states as a
class. Separately, the **Fifth and Sixth Schedules** govern the administration
of **scheduled areas and tribal areas** within states.

> [!exam] **'Territory of India' is wider than 'Union of India'.** The Union of India includes only the **states** — the members of the federal system, sharing a distribution of powers with the Centre. The Territory of India includes the states **plus** union territories **plus** acquired territories, which are **directly administered by the Centre**.

**Acquiring foreign territory.** Being a sovereign state, India may acquire
foreign territory by the modes recognised in international law:

- **Cession** — following a treaty, purchase, gift, lease or plebiscite
- **Occupation** — of territory not held by a recognised ruler
- **Conquest or subjugation**

India has used these since 1950 to acquire **Dadra and Nagar Haveli**;
**Goa, Daman and Diu**; **Puducherry**; and **Sikkim**.

:::cloze
The three categories of Indian territory: territories of the {{states}}, {{union territories}}, and territories that may be {{acquired}}.
Names and extent are listed in the {{First Schedule}}.
:::

## Articles 2 and 3 — two different powers

These two Articles are constantly swapped in questions. The dividing line is
whether the territory is **already inside** the Union.

| | **Article 2** | **Article 3** |
|---|---|---|
| Deals with | States **outside** the Union | States **already in** the Union |
| Power | **Admit** or **establish** new states | **Form**, alter, rename existing states |
| Nature | External expansion | Internal re-adjustment |

Article 2 grants Parliament **two** powers, on "such terms and conditions as it
thinks fit": to **admit** states that **already exist**, and to **establish**
states that **did not exist before**.

Article 3 authorises Parliament to:

1. **form a new state** — by separating territory from a state, uniting two or
   more states or parts of states, or uniting any territory to a part of a state
2. **increase** the area of any state
3. **diminish** the area of any state
4. **alter the boundaries** of any state
5. **alter the name** of any state

The power to form new states also covers forming a new state **or union
territory** by uniting a part of any state or union territory to any other.

> [!warn] Article 2 = admission/establishment of states **not yet in** the Union. Article 3 = reorganisation **within** the Union. Sikkim's admission ran on Article 2's logic; Telangana's creation on Article 3.

:::cloze
{{Article 2}} empowers Parliament to admit or establish new states not already part of the Union.
{{Article 3}} covers the formation of new states and alteration of areas, boundaries or {{names}} of existing states.
:::

## The two conditions on Article 3 — and how weak they are

A bill under Article 3 carries **two** procedural conditions:

1. It can be introduced in Parliament **only with the prior recommendation of
   the President**.
2. **Before** recommending it, the President must **refer it to the legislature
   of the state concerned** for its views, within a **specified period**.

Every one of those safeguards then dissolves:

- The President (or Parliament) is **not bound** by the state legislature's
  views, and may **accept or reject** them — even when they arrive in time.
- If the bill is later **amended** in Parliament, **no fresh reference** to the
  state legislature is needed.
- For a **union territory**, **no reference at all** is required — Parliament
  acts as it deems fit.

So Parliament can form new states, or alter the areas, boundaries and names of
existing ones, **without their consent**. It can redraw the political map of
India at will, and the **territorial integrity or continued existence of a state
is not guaranteed** by the Constitution.

> [!exam] The contrast that gets asked: India is **"an indestructible Union of destructible states"**; the USA is **"an indestructible Union of indestructible states."** In the USA the federal government cannot form new states or alter existing borders **without the consent of the states concerned**.

**Article 4** completes the picture. Laws made under **Article 2** (admission or
establishment) and **Article 3** (formation, alteration, renaming) are declared
**not to be amendments of the Constitution under Article 368** — even though
they change the **First and Fourth Schedules**. They therefore pass by a
**simple majority** through the **ordinary legislative process**.

:::cloze
A bill under Article 3 needs the prior recommendation of the {{President}}, who must first refer it to the {{state legislature}} — whose views are {{not binding}}.
Under {{Article 4}}, such a law is not an amendment under Article 368 and passes by a {{simple majority}}.
:::

## Berubari, and what Article 3 cannot do

The Central Government's decision to cede part of the **Berubari Union (West
Bengal)** to **Pakistan** caused political agitation, and in **1960** the
President made a reference to the **Supreme Court**.

The Court held that the Article 3 power to **diminish the area of a state**
does **not** cover **cession of Indian territory to a foreign country**. Indian
territory can be ceded only by **amending the Constitution under Article 368** —
so the **9th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1960** was enacted to make the
transfer.

In **1969**, the Supreme Court drew the other half of the line: **settling a
boundary dispute** with another country needs **no constitutional amendment**.
It can be done by **executive action**, because it involves no cession of
territory.

> [!warn] Cession of territory ⇒ **Article 368 amendment**. Settlement of a boundary dispute ⇒ **executive action**. The distinction is whether Indian territory actually changes hands.

## Exchange of territories with Bangladesh — the 100th Amendment

The **100th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2015** gave effect to the India–
Bangladesh land boundary deal. Under it:

- India **transferred 111 enclaves** to Bangladesh; Bangladesh **transferred 51
  enclaves** to India.
- The deal also covered **adverse possessions** and the demarcation of a
  **6.1 km** undemarcated stretch.
- For these purposes the amendment modified the **First Schedule** entries of
  **four states — Assam, West Bengal, Meghalaya and Tripura**.

The India–Bangladesh land boundary runs about **4,096.7 km**. Its tangled
history is a chain of failed settlements: the **Radcliffe Award (1947)** fixed
the India–East Pakistan line, disputes were taken up by the **Bagge Award
(1950)**, then the **Nehru–Noon Agreement (1958)** — and the Berubari question
went to the Supreme Court out of that. The **9th Amendment (1960)** could not be
notified for the territories in former East Pakistan. The **Land Boundary
Agreement of 16 May 1974** was never ratified because it involved transfer of
territory requiring an amendment; the **Protocol of 6 September 2011**, prepared
with the concurrence of the four state governments, finally identified the
enclaves, adverse possessions and undemarcated stretch.

:::cloze
The {{100th}} Amendment Act, {{2015}} implemented the India–Bangladesh land boundary deal.
India transferred {{111}} enclaves to Bangladesh; Bangladesh transferred {{51}} enclaves to India.
:::

## Integration of the princely states

At independence India held two kinds of political units: the **British
provinces**, under direct British rule, and the **princely states**, ruled by
native princes under the **paramountcy of the British Crown**.

The **Indian Independence Act, 1947** created the two dominions and gave the
princely states **three options**: join India, join Pakistan, or stay
independent. Of the **552** princely states inside India's geographical
boundaries, **549 joined** India. The remaining **three refused** — and each was
brought in differently:

| State | How it was integrated |
|---|---|
| **Hyderabad** | **Police action** |
| **Junagarh** | **Referendum** |
| **Kashmir** | **Instrument of Accession** |

**The 1950 map.** The Constitution originally used a **four-fold
classification**, **29 units** in all:

| Class | Count | What it was |
|---|---|---|
| **Part A** | **9** | Erstwhile governor's provinces of British India |
| **Part B** | **9** | Erstwhile princely states with legislatures |
| **Part C** | **10** | Chief commissioner's provinces + some princely states, **centrally administered** |
| **Part D** | **1** | **Andaman and Nicobar Islands** — the sole Part D territory |

## Reorganisation on language — Dhar, JVP, Fazl Ali

The integration of the princely states was purely an **ad hoc** arrangement,
and demands for reorganisation on a **linguistic basis** grew, particularly in
South India.

- **Dhar Commission** — the **Linguistic Provinces Commission** appointed in
  **June 1948** under **S.K. Dhar**. Reported **December 1948** and recommended
  reorganisation on **administrative convenience**, **not** language. This
  caused much resentment.
- **JVP Committee** — appointed by the **Congress in December 1948** to
  re-examine the question: **Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel and Pattabhi
  Sitaramayya**. It had **no chairman or convenor**. Reported **April 1949** and
  **formally rejected language** as the basis of reorganisation.

Both reports were overtaken by events. In **October 1953** the Government of
India was forced to create the **first linguistic state — Andhra** — by
separating the **Telugu-speaking areas from Madras state**, after a prolonged
agitation and the death of **Potti Sriramulu** following a **56-day hunger
strike**. Under the **Andhra State Act, 1953**, **Kurnool** was the capital and
the state **high court was at Guntur**.

**Fazl Ali Commission.** Andhra intensified demands elsewhere, so in **December
1953** a **three-member States Reorganisation Commission** was appointed under
**Fazl Ali**, with **K.M. Panikkar** and **H.N. Kunzru**. It reported in
**September 1955** and **broadly accepted language** as the basis — but
**rejected the theory of 'one language–one state'**, holding that the **unity of
India** must be the primary consideration. It identified **four factors**:

1. Preservation and strengthening of the **unity and security** of the country
2. **Linguistic and cultural homogeneity**
3. **Financial, economic and administrative** considerations
4. **Welfare of the people** of each state and of the nation as a whole

The Commission recommended abolishing the four-fold classification and creating
**16 states and 3 centrally administered territories**. The government accepted
with **minor modifications**: by the **States Reorganisation Act, 1956** and the
**7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956**, the **Part A / Part B distinction
was abolished** and **Part C states were abolished** — some merged into adjacent
states, others made union territories. The result, on **1 November 1956**, was
**14 states and 6 union territories**.

> [!exam] Three number traps in one paragraph: recommended **16 states + 3 territories** → created **14 states + 6 UTs**; commission of **3 members**; report **September 1955**, Act **1956**.

The six union territories of 1956 were **Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Delhi,
Himachal Pradesh, Laccadive–Minicoy–Amindivi Islands, Manipur and Tripura**.

The 1956 Act also **created Kerala** (Travancore–Cochin + Malabar district of
Madras + Kasargode of South Canara), **enlarged Andhra Pradesh** (Telugu areas
of Hyderabad state merged into Andhra), folded **Madhya Bharat, Vindhya Pradesh
and Bhopal** into **Madhya Pradesh**, **Saurashtra and Kutch** into **Bombay**,
**Coorg** into **Mysore**, **PEPSU** into **Punjab**, and **Ajmer** into
**Rajasthan**.

:::cloze
The {{Dhar}} Commission (1948) recommended reorganisation on {{administrative convenience}}; the {{JVP}} Committee (1949) rejected language altogether.
The {{Fazl Ali}} Commission accepted language but rejected the theory of {{one language–one state}}.
:::

## The map after 1956 — states 15 to 29

Reorganisation did not stop in 1956. Learn this as an **ordinal chain**, because
that is how it is asked.

| Year | State (number) | Carved from |
|---|---|---|
| **1960** | **Gujarat (15th)** | Bilingual **Bombay** split into Maharashtra + Gujarat |
| **1963** | **Nagaland (16th)** | Naga Hills + Tuensang, out of **Assam** |
| **1966** | **Haryana (17th)** | **Punjab**, on the **Shah Commission (1966)** report |
| **1971** | **Himachal Pradesh (18th)** | UT elevated to statehood |
| **1972** | **Manipur (19th), Tripura (20th), Meghalaya (21st)** | UTs and a sub-state of **Assam** |
| **1975** | **Sikkim (22nd)** | Former protectorate |
| **1987** | **Mizoram (23rd), Arunachal Pradesh (24th), Goa (25th)** | UTs |
| **2000** | **Chhattisgarh (26th), Uttarakhand (27th), Jharkhand (28th)** | **MP**, **UP**, **Bihar** |
| **2014** | **Telangana (29th)** | **Andhra Pradesh** |

**Nagaland** was created to satisfy the movement of the hostile Nagas; before
statehood it was placed under the **Governor of Assam in 1961**.

**Haryana** followed the demand for a separate **'Sikh Homeland' (Punjabi
Subha)** raised by the **Akali Dal** under **Master Tara Singh**. The **Shah
Commission (1966)** split Punjab three ways: **Punjabi-speaking areas** into
Punjab, **Hindi-speaking areas** into **Haryana**, and the **hill areas** into
the UT of **Himachal Pradesh** — with **Chandigarh** made a **union territory
and the common capital** of both.

**Meghalaya** came in two steps: the **22nd Amendment Act, 1969** first made it
an **'autonomous state' or 'sub-state' within Assam**, with its own legislature
and council of ministers. That did not satisfy its people, and full statehood
followed in **1972**, when the UTs of **Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh**
(originally **NEFA — North-East Frontier Agency**) were also carved out of
Assam.

**Mizoram**'s statehood in 1987 followed the **Mizoram Peace Accord (1986)**
between the Centre and the **Mizo National Front**, ending a two-decade
insurgency.

**Sikkim** is the outlier, and a favourite. It was a princely state ruled by the
**Chogyal** until 1947, then became a **protectorate of India** — India taking
responsibility for its **defence, external affairs and communications**. In
1974 Sikkim sought closer association, and the **35th Amendment Act, 1974**
created a **new class of statehood**: **'associate state'**, inserting
**Article 2-A** and a **new 10th Schedule**. It did not last. A **referendum in
1975** voted to abolish the **Chogyal** and make Sikkim part of India, so the
**36th Amendment Act, 1975** made it a **full state (the 22nd)**, added
**Article 371-F**, and **repealed Article 2-A and the 10th Schedule**.

:::cloze
Sikkim became an {{associate state}} by the {{35th}} Amendment Act, 1974, which inserted Article {{2-A}}.
The {{36th}} Amendment Act, 1975 made it a full state and added Article {{371-F}}.
:::

## The acquired union territories

- **Dadra and Nagar Haveli** — Portuguese-ruled until its **liberation in 1954**,
  then administered until 1961 by an administrator **chosen by the people
  themselves**; made a UT by the **10th Amendment Act, 1961**.
- **Goa, Daman and Diu** — taken from the Portuguese by **police action in
  1961**; made a UT by the **12th Amendment Act, 1962**. **Goa became a state in
  1987**, leaving **Daman and Diu** as a separate UT.
- **Puducherry** — the former **French** establishments of **Puducherry,
  Karaikal, Mahe and Yanam**, handed over in **1954**, administered as an
  **'acquired territory'** until made a UT by the **14th Amendment Act, 1962**.
- In **2020**, the UTs of **Dadra and Nagar Haveli** and **Daman and Diu**
  merged into one UT — **Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu** — under the
  **Merger of Union Territories Act, 2019**.

> [!tip] Three amendments, three territories, in order: **10th → Dadra and Nagar Haveli (1961)**, **12th → Goa, Daman and Diu (1962)**, **14th → Puducherry (1962)**.

## Jammu and Kashmir, and the present map

Until **2019** the erstwhile **State of Jammu and Kashmir** had its **own
constitution** and a special status under **Article 370**. In 2019 that status
was abolished by **The Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order,
2019**, which **superseded the 1954 Order** and extended **all provisions** of
the Constitution of India to Jammu and Kashmir.

The **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019** then **bifurcated the state
into two union territories**: the **UT of Jammu and Kashmir** and the **UT of
Ladakh**, the latter comprising the **Kargil and Leh** districts.

**The present count: 28 states and 8 union territories.**

The **eight union territories** are: **Andaman and Nicobar Islands ·
Chandigarh · Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu · Delhi (National Capital
Territory) · Jammu and Kashmir · Ladakh · Lakshadweep · Puducherry**.

> [!exam] Telangana was the **29th state**, yet India has **28 states** today — because **Jammu and Kashmir stopped being a state in 2019**. The ordinal and the count are different numbers, and the question usually mixes them.

**Changes of name.** **United Provinces → Uttar Pradesh (1950)** was the
**first**. Then **Madras → Tamil Nadu (1969)**, **Mysore → Karnataka (1973)**,
**Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindivi Islands → Lakshadweep (1973)**, **Uttaranchal
→ Uttarakhand (2006)**, **Pondicherry → Puducherry (2006)**, **Orissa → Odisha
(2011)**. Separately, the **69th Amendment Act, 1991** redesignated the UT of
Delhi as the **National Capital Territory of Delhi in 1992** — **without**
conferring full statehood.

:::cloze
The first state to be renamed was {{United Provinces}}, which became {{Uttar Pradesh}} in {{1950}}.
The {{69th}} Amendment Act, 1991 made Delhi the National Capital Territory — without full {{statehood}}.
:::

## Laws made under Article 3 — the ones that get asked

Thirty-three Acts have been passed under Article 3. These are the ones worth
knowing by name:

| Act | What it did |
|---|---|
| **Andhra State Act, 1953** | Created the **first linguistic state** from Telugu areas of Madras |
| **States Reorganisation Act, 1956** | **14 states + 6 UTs**; created **Kerala**, enlarged **Andhra Pradesh** |
| **Bombay Reorganisation Act, 1960** | Created **Gujarat (15th)**; renamed the rest **Maharashtra** |
| **State of Nagaland Act, 1962** | Created **Nagaland (16th)** from the Naga Hills–Tuensang area |
| **Punjab Reorganisation Act, 1966** | Created **Haryana (17th)** and the UT of **Chandigarh** |
| **Assam Reorganisation (Meghalaya) Act, 1969** | Made Meghalaya an **autonomous sub-state** in Assam |
| **State of Himachal Pradesh Act, 1970** | Elevated the UT to a **state (18th)** |
| **North-Eastern Areas (Reorganisation) Act, 1971** | **Manipur (19th), Tripura (20th), Meghalaya (21st)**; UTs of **Mizoram** and **Arunachal Pradesh** |
| **Goa, Daman and Diu Reorganisation Act, 1987** | Created the state of **Goa (25th)** |
| **MP / UP / Bihar Reorganisation Acts, 2000** | **Chhattisgarh (26th), Uttaranchal (27th), Jharkhand (28th)** |
| **Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014** | Created **Telangana (29th)** |
| **J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019** | Split the state into the **UTs of J&K and Ladakh** |

Two more are worth a line each because they are unusual: the **Assam
(Alteration of Boundaries) Act, 1951** ceded a strip of Assam **to Bhutan**, and
the **Acquired Territories (Merger) Act, 1960** merged into **Assam, Punjab and
West Bengal** certain territories acquired from **Pakistan** under the 1958 and
1959 agreements.

:::recall In one line — why is India called "an indestructible Union of destructible states", and which single case marks the outer limit of that destructibility?
Parliament can create, merge, shrink or rename any state by a **simple majority** under **Articles 3 and 4**, without the state's consent — but the **Berubari Union case (1960)** fixed the limit: that power **cannot cede Indian territory to a foreign country**, which requires an **Article 368 amendment**. States are destructible **internally**; the Union's external boundary is not Parliament's to give away by ordinary law.
:::
