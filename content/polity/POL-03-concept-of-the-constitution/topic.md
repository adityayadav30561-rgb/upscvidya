# Concept of the Constitution

The last two chapters were history — what the British built, and who wrote the
document that replaced it. This chapter steps back and asks a flatter question:
**what is a constitution at all**, and what makes one good?

It looks like the softest chapter in Part I, and that is exactly why it is
mis-prepared. It carries almost no dates, so there is nothing to cram — but it
is dense with **name-to-idea pairs**, and those are the easiest thing in the
world to set a question on. Bryce gave one classification, Wheare a definition
and a label for India, Bulmer a list of functions and a pair of archetypes,
Henkin a list of eight elements. Attach every idea in this chapter to its owner
as you read.

There is a second thread worth watching. Every classification below ends with
the same awkward sentence: **India does not sit cleanly on either side.** Written
but partly conventional, rigid in places and flexible in others, federal in form
and unitary in emergency. That refusal to fit is not sloppiness — it is the
subject of Chapter 4, and this chapter is where the vocabulary for it is built.

:::predict Of the classifications in this chapter, two produce a famous verdict on India that examiners ask constantly. Which two categories does the Indian Constitution refuse to fit, and what is it called instead?
**Rigid versus flexible**, and **federal versus unitary**. India is neither rigid nor flexible but a **synthesis of both**, and because it carries federal and unitary provisions together, **K.C. Wheare** described it as **quasi-federal**.
:::

## What the word means

'Constitution' comes from the Latin **constituere** — 'to establish' or 'to set
up'. In its modern sense a constitution is a set of principles that specify two
things: the **organisation and working of the government**, and the
**relationship between the government and the people** in terms of their rights
and duties.

The same object is described by many labels, and any of them can appear in an
option: the **fundamental law of the land**, the **supreme law of the state**,
the **basic law of the country**, the **instrument of the government**, the
**rules of the state**, the **basic structure of the polity**, and the
**grundnorm** of the country.

> [!note] Two elements are doing the work in every definition below: the
> **organisation of government** (who holds which power) and the
> **government–people relationship** (rights and duties). A definition missing
> one of the two is incomplete.

Four definitions are standard, and each emphasises something slightly different:

| Scholar | What the definition stresses |
|---|---|
| **Gilchrist** | Rules determining the organisation of government, the **distribution of powers** among its organs, and the principles for exercising them |
| **Gettell** | The **fundamental principles that determine the form of a state** — its organisation, the distribution of sovereign power, the scope of functions, and the government–people relation |
| **Wheare** | The **whole system of government** — the collection of rules that establish and regulate the government |
| **Wade and Phillips** | A **document having a special legal sanctity** that sets out the framework and principal functions of the organs of the state |

Note the odd one out: **Wade and Phillips** is the only definition that insists
the constitution be a **document** with special legal sanctity. On that
definition the United Kingdom, whose constitution is not a document at all,
would barely have one.

:::cloze

{{Wheare}} defined the constitution as the whole system of government — the collection of rules that establish and regulate the government.
The definition insisting on a document with special legal sanctity is that of {{Wade and Phillips|Wade & Phillips}}, and a constitution is also called the {{grundnorm}} of the country.
:::

## What a constitution does

**Elliot Bulmer** set out the functions, or purposes, of a constitution.
His list is the one to quote, and it has **eight** items:

1. Declare and define the **boundaries** of the political community.
2. Declare and define the **nature and authority** of the political community.
3. Express the **identity and values** of a national community.
4. Declare and define the **rights and duties of citizens**.
5. Establish and regulate the **political institutions** of the community.
6. **Divide or share power** between different layers of government or
   sub-state communities.
7. Declare the **official religious identity** of the state and demarcate
   relations between sacred and secular authority.
8. Commit the state to particular **social, economic or developmental goals**.

Two of these deserve a second look, because India's Constitution answers them in
its own way. On function 7 — religious identity — India declares itself
**secular**, which is a declaration by refusal. On function 8 — developmental
goals — the **Directive Principles** are precisely that commitment, made
non-justiciable so that the goal binds policy without binding the courts.

## What makes a constitution good

Seven qualities are listed, and they are best learnt as **three that pull
towards restraint and four that pull towards use**:

1. **Brevity** — precise, with no unwanted provisions. A lengthy constitution
   crowded with detail creates confusion in interpreting its clauses.
2. **Clarity** — provisions in clear terms; complicated language reduces
   understanding.
3. **Definiteness** — a definite meaning for each provision. Ambiguity
   **increases the discretion of judges** in interpretation.
4. **Comprehensiveness** — it must lay down the powers of government *and* the
   rights and duties of citizens, which **reduces controversies and
   litigation**.
5. **Suitability** — it must reflect the needs and aspirations of the people and
   suit the historical, socio-cultural, economic and political conditions of the
   nation.
6. **Stability** — it should support political stability and not allow easy
   tampering, which strengthens citizens' obedience to it.
7. **Adaptability** — it should be dynamic, not static; able to adapt to
   changing situations. A **living document**.

> [!warn] **Brevity and comprehensiveness pull against each other**, and so do
> **stability and adaptability**. A good constitution is a balance between each
> pair, not a maximum of any one — which is why the Indian Constitution, the
> longest in the world, forfeits brevity deliberately to buy comprehensiveness.

{{Adaptability}} requires a constitution to be dynamic rather than static — a {{living document}}.
:::

## How constitutions are classified

Five classifications, each built on a different test. Learn the **basis** of each
classification, not just the two names — the basis is what half the questions
actually ask.

### 1. Evolved and enacted — the basis is *evolution*

An **evolved** constitution is the outcome of a slow, gradual evolutionary
process. Its provisions live in **conventions, practices, principles and
judicial decisions**. It is also called a **cumulative** constitution. The
**British Constitution** is the standard example.

An **enacted** constitution is deliberately made — by a constituent assembly or
a constitutional council, or promulgated by a king or a parliament. Its
provisions sit in a book, a document or a series of documents. It is also called
a **conventional** constitution, and the **American and Indian** Constitutions
are the examples.

> [!tip] The vocabulary inverts what you expect. The **cumulative** constitution
> (British) is the one full of *conventions*, while the **conventional**
> constitution (American, Indian) is the one that is written down. Learn this
> pair deliberately — it is a favourite trick.

### 2. Written and unwritten — the basis is *incorporation of provisions*

A **written** constitution has its provisions incorporated in a book or document,
consciously formulated and adopted by a constituent assembly or constitutional
convention. Also called **documentary** or **codified**. Examples: the **USA,
Canada, Japan, France and India**.

An **unwritten** constitution has most of its provisions *not* so incorporated —
they are found in conventions, practices, principles, charters, statutes and
judicial decisions, as a product of historical evolution rather than deliberate
formulation. Also called **non-documentary** or **uncodified**. Examples: the
**UK, New Zealand and Israel**.

The written/unwritten split is more or less the same distinction as the
evolved/enacted split, approached from a different angle.

### 3. Rigid and flexible — the basis is *the method of amendment*

This classification was given by **Lord James Bryce**.

A **rigid** constitution cannot be amended in the same manner as ordinary laws;
it requires a **special procedure**. It therefore **makes a distinction between
constitutional law and ordinary law**. Also called **inelastic**. Examples: the
**USA, Australia and Switzerland**.

A **flexible** constitution can be amended in the same manner as ordinary laws,
with no special procedure, and so **makes no distinction between constitutional
law and ordinary law**. Also called **elastic**. Examples: the **UK and New
Zealand**.

**The Constitution of India is neither rigid nor flexible — it is a synthesis of
both.**

### 4. Federal and unitary — the basis is *national–regional relations*

A **federal** constitution provides for a **division of power** between the
national government and the regional governments, each operating independently
in its own jurisdiction. Found in the **USA, Switzerland, Australia, Canada,
Russia and Brazil**.

A **unitary** constitution provides for the **concentration of power** in the
national government; regional governments act as its **subordinate agencies**
and exercise only such powers as are delegated to them. Found in the **UK,
France, Japan, China, Italy and Norway**.

The Constitution of India contains **both federal and unitary provisions**, which
is why **K.C. Wheare** described it as **quasi-federal**.

### 5. Procedural and prescriptive — the basis is *the purpose of the document*

These two archetypes are also **Elliot Bulmer's**, and the distinction is about
what the document is *for*.

A **procedural** constitution defines the legal and political structures of
public institutions and sets the legal limits of government power, in order to
protect democratic processes and fundamental human rights. It is a rulebook for
how power is used.

A **prescriptive** constitution does all that *and more*: it assumes — or
attempts to impose — a **broad consensus on common societal goals** that public
authorities must strive to achieve. It is a rulebook plus a destination.

> [!exam] Three names carry the classifications, and swapping them is the whole
> question. **Bryce** → rigid and flexible. **Wheare** → *quasi-federal* (his
> verdict on India, not a classification). **Bulmer** → procedural and
> prescriptive, and the eight functions.

## Constitution is not the same as constitutionalism

Here is the conceptual heart of the chapter. Having a constitution is a fact
about a country's paperwork. **Constitutionalism** is a fact about how power
actually behaves.

**A country may have a constitution and still not have constitutionalism** — a
dictatorship is the standing example. Unlimited power jeopardises the freedom of
the people, and absolute power corrupts absolutely; so a constitution must be
**permeated with** constitutionalism rather than merely coexist with it.

Four propositions carry the concept:

1. **Constitutionalism means limited government.** It is the **antithesis of
   arbitrary power**.
2. A country has constitutionalism only when its constitution seeks to
   **decentralise power instead of concentrating it at one point**, and imposes
   further restraints on it.
3. It describes a polity governed **under** a constitution that ordains limited
   government and the **rule of law**, as opposed to arbitrary, despotic,
   authoritarian or totalitarian rule. Constitutional government must
   necessarily be **democratic** government — and **arbitrary power negates
   constitutionalism even when a constitutional document confers it**.
4. It wants a political order in which the powers of government are limited —
   another name for a **limited and therefore "civilised" government**, one in
   which those who govern must conform to laws and rules.

> [!exam] Point 3 is the trap the paper actually sets: a power can be **granted
> by the constitution itself** and still be a negation of constitutionalism.
> Legality is not the test — **limitation** is.

Four definitions are standard here too:

| Scholar | Definition of constitutionalism / constitutional government |
|---|---|
| **Friedrich** | A system of **effective restraints upon governmental action**; a body of rules ensuring fair play, thus rendering government **responsible** |
| **Roucek** | **Essentially limited government** — the antithesis of government by the unrestrained will of rulers |
| **Wheare** | **More than government according to the terms of a constitution**: government by *rule* as opposed to arbitrary government, limited by the constitution and not merely by the desires and capacities of those in power |
| **Ybema** | A government is constitutional **only when the rulers are subject to rules and principles limiting their power**; the antithesis of arbitrary rule |

:::cloze

A country may possess a {{constitution}} without possessing constitutionalism, as in a {{dictatorship}}.
{{Friedrich}} defined it as a system of effective restraints upon governmental action, while {{Roucek}} called it essentially limited government.
:::

## The eight elements of constitutionalism

**Louis Henkin** identified **eight** elements — the features or principles that
together make constitutionalism real rather than declared:

1. **Popular sovereignty**
2. **Rule of law**
3. **Democratic government** — responsible and accountable
4. **Separation of powers** — checks and balances
5. **An independent judiciary**
6. **Civilian control of the military**
7. **Police governed by law and subject to judicial control**
8. **Respect for individual rights**

Read the list as an answer to a single question: *what stops power?* Elements 1
to 3 say power must come from and answer to the people; 4 and 5 split power and
put a neutral umpire over it; 6 and 7 subordinate the two institutions that hold
actual force — the army and the police — to civilian and judicial authority; 8
sets the floor nothing may cross.

> [!note] Two lists of **eight** sit in this chapter and are routinely swapped:
> **Bulmer's eight functions of a constitution** and **Henkin's eight elements
> of constitutionalism**. Bulmer describes what the document *does*; Henkin
> describes what a state must *have*.

:::cloze
{{Louis Henkin|Henkin}} identified {{8|eight}} elements of constitutionalism, beginning with popular sovereignty and the {{rule of law}}.
Two of the elements subordinate the institutions holding actual force: {{civilian}} control of the military, and police governed by law and subject to {{judicial}} control.
The archetypes of {{procedural}} and {{prescriptive}} constitutions were given by Elliot Bulmer.
:::

:::recall In one line — a state has a written constitution, and every power its ruler exercises is expressly granted by that document. Does it have constitutionalism, and why?
**Not necessarily** — constitutionalism is about **limitation, not legality**. Arbitrary power negates constitutionalism even when a constitutional document confers it, so a constitution that concentrates power instead of decentralising and restraining it gives a country a constitution without constitutionalism.
:::
