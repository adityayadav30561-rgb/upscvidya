# Making of the Constitution

The last chapter ended with a machinery of government built by somebody else —
174 years of British statutes culminating in the Government of India Act, 1935.
This chapter is about the body that finally took the pen out of Westminster's
hand: the **Constituent Assembly**, which sat from **December 1946** and finished
its work on **26 November 1949**.

Two things make it awkward, and both are examined constantly. First, the Assembly
was **not** created by Indians — it was constituted under a British scheme, the
**Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946**, and its members were **indirectly elected** by
provincial legislators, not by the people. Second, it changed shape mid-way:
Partition cut its strength from **389 to 299** and simultaneously handed it a
second job it was never designed for.

So read the chapter around three questions: **who sat in it**, **what powers it
had**, and **how long it took**. Almost every question ever asked on this topic
is one of those three wearing a date.

:::predict The Constitution was passed on 26 November 1949, but the Republic dates from 26 January 1950. Why the fourteen-month gap — and was nothing at all in force in between?
**Fifteen Articles came into force on 26 November 1949 itself** — those on citizenship, elections, the provisional Parliament and the transitional provisions (Articles **5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 60, 324, 366, 367, 379, 380, 388, 391, 392 and 393**). The rest waited for **26 January 1950** purely for symbolism: the Congress had celebrated **Purna Swaraj Day on 26 January 1930**, and the framers wanted the Republic to begin on that anniversary.
:::

## Where the demand came from

The idea of Indians writing their own constitution was not a 1946 invention. It
has a traceable line, and the line is a favourite chronology question.

- **1934** — first proposed by **M.N. Roy**, a pioneer of the communist movement
  in India. Note that the demand originates outside the Congress.
- **1935** — the **Indian National Congress** officially demanded a Constituent
  Assembly.
- **1938** — **Jawaharlal Nehru**, on behalf of the Congress, declared that the
  constitution of free India must be framed by a Constituent Assembly elected on
  **adult franchise**, without outside interference. The Congress repeated this
  as a formal resolution in **1939**.
- **1940** — the **August Offer** saw the British government accept the demand
  **in principle** for the first time.
- **1942** — **Sir Stafford Cripps** came with a draft for an independent
  constitution to be framed after the war. The **Muslim League** rejected it: it
  wanted India divided into two autonomous states with **two separate
  Constituent Assemblies**.
- **1946** — the **Cabinet Mission** rejected the two-assemblies idea outright,
  but produced a scheme that satisfied the Muslim League "to a certain extent".
  That scheme is what actually created the Assembly.

> [!note] The demand was conceded in principle in 1940 (August Offer), but the
> Assembly itself was constituted only under the Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946.
> Accepting a demand and executing it are six years apart.

:::cloze
The demand for a Constituent Assembly was first put forward in {{1934}} by {{M.N. Roy}}, and the Congress officially adopted it in {{1935}}.
The British government accepted the demand in principle through the {{August Offer}} of {{1940}}.
The {{Muslim League}} rejected the Cripps Proposals of 1942 because it wanted {{two}} separate Constituent Assemblies.
:::

## Who sat in the Assembly

The Assembly was constituted in **November 1946** under the Cabinet Mission Plan.
Its arithmetic is the single most examined block in this chapter, so learn it as
a sum, not as loose numbers.

**Total strength: 389.**

| Component | Seats |
|---|---|
| 11 Governors' Provinces | 292 |
| 4 Chief Commissioners' Provinces | 4 |
| **British India subtotal** | **296** |
| Princely states | 93 |
| **Total** | **389** |

Three features of the scheme decide everything that is later said for and
against the Assembly:

1. **Seats were allotted by population** — roughly **one seat per million**
   people. This was a proportionality rule, not a democratic one.
2. **Provincial seats were divided among three communities** — **Muslims, Sikhs
   and General** — in proportion to their populations. Nobody else got a
   communal quota.
3. **The method of choosing differed by unit.** Provincial representatives were
   **elected by the members of the provincial legislative assembly**, by
   **proportional representation with a single transferable vote**. Princely
   state representatives were simply **nominated by the rulers**.

Put those together and you get the description the exam wants: the Assembly was
**partly elected and partly nominated**, and even the elected part was
**indirectly elected**.

Elections to the **296** British-India seats were held in **July–August 1946**.
The Congress won **208**, the Muslim League **73**, and the remaining **15** went
to small parties and independents. The **93** princely-state seats stayed
**vacant** — the states initially stayed away from the Assembly.

By community, the 296 broke down as **163 Hindus, 80 Muslims, 31 Scheduled
Castes, 6 Indian Christians, 6 Backward Tribes, 4 Sikhs, 3 Anglo-Indians and 3
Parsees**. The Assembly also included **15 women**, among them **Ammu
Swaminathan**, **Sarojini Naidu**, **Sucheta Kripalani**, **Hansa Mehta** and
**Vijayalakshmi Pandit**.

Despite the indirect method, the Assembly did contain representatives of every
significant section of Indian society — every religion, every major party, and
the depressed classes. The famous absentee is **Mahatma Gandhi**, who was never
a member of the Constituent Assembly.

> [!exam] Three numbers get swapped in options: **389** (original strength),
> **296** (British India seats actually contested in 1946) and **299** (strength
> after Partition). If a question mentions the July–August 1946 elections, the
> denominator is **296**, not 389.

:::cloze
Under the Cabinet Mission Plan the total strength of the Constituent Assembly was {{389}}, of which {{296}} were allotted to British India and {{93}} to the princely states.
Provincial representatives were elected by members of the provincial legislative assemblies using proportional representation by {{single transferable vote}}, while princely-state representatives were {{nominated}} by the rulers.
In the 1946 elections the Congress won {{208}} seats and the Muslim League won {{73}}.
:::

## The Assembly at work

The Assembly held its **first meeting on 9 December 1946**. The Muslim League
**boycotted** it and pressed its demand for a separate Pakistan, so only **207**
members attended.

Following the French practice, the **oldest member** took the chair first:
**Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha** was elected **temporary Chairman**. Two days later,
on **11 December 1946**, **Dr. Rajendra Prasad** was elected **permanent
President** of the Assembly. **H.C. Mukherjee** was elected **Vice-President** on
**25 January 1947**, and a second Vice-President, **V.T. Krishnamachari**, was
elected on **16 July 1947**. **Sir B.N. Rau** was appointed **constitutional
adviser** to the Assembly.

### The Objectives Resolution

On **13 December 1946**, Nehru moved the **Objectives Resolution** — the
document that fixed the philosophy of everything the Assembly would later draft.
It declared India an **Independent Sovereign Republic**, promised justice,
equality and liberty to all its people, and guaranteed adequate safeguards for
minorities, backward and tribal areas, and depressed classes. It was
**unanimously adopted on 22 January 1947**.

This resolution is not a piece of trivia. Its modified version became the
**Preamble** of the Constitution — which is why the Preamble is treated as a
statement of the Assembly's intent rather than as ordinary text.

> [!exam] The date pair is the trap: **moved 13 December 1946**, **adopted
> 22 January 1947**. Both dates appear as options in the same question.

:::cloze
The Objectives Resolution was moved by {{Nehru|Jawaharlal Nehru}} on {{13 December 1946}} and was unanimously adopted on {{22 January 1947}}.
Its modified version was later adopted as the {{Preamble}} of the Constitution.
At the first meeting on {{9 December 1946}}, {{Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha|Sachchidananda Sinha}} presided as temporary Chairman, and {{Dr. Rajendra Prasad|Rajendra Prasad}} became permanent President on 11 December 1946.
:::

## What Partition changed

The princely states came in slowly. Representatives of **six states** — Baroda,
Bikaner, Jaipur, Patiala, Rewa and Udaipur — joined by **28 April 1947**. Once
the **Mountbatten Plan of 3 June 1947** settled Partition, the rest of the states
took their seats too, and the Muslim League members from the areas remaining in
the Indian Dominion also joined.

The **Indian Independence Act, 1947** then made **three changes** to the
Assembly, and all three are examinable:

1. **The Assembly became a fully sovereign body.** It could now frame any
   constitution it pleased, and could alter or abrogate any law made by the
   British Parliament as it applied to India. The charge that the Assembly was
   not sovereign dies here.
2. **The Assembly became a legislative body as well** — a dual role, discharged
   in two separate sittings. As a **constituent body** it was chaired by
   **Dr. Rajendra Prasad**; as the **Dominion Legislature** it was chaired by
   **G.V. Mavalankar**, elected **Speaker on 17 November 1947**. The two
   functions continued until **26 November 1949**.
3. **The Muslim League members from Pakistan areas withdrew** — West Punjab,
   East Bengal, the North-West Frontier Province, Sindh, Baluchistan and Sylhet —
   and a separate Constituent Assembly was set up for Pakistan.

The withdrawal produced the second set of numbers you must hold:

Strength **389 → 299**. Provinces **296 → 229**; princely states **93 → 70**.

After the Constitution commenced, the same body carried on as the **Provisional
Parliament** from **26 January 1950** until the first elected Parliament was
constituted after the **1951–52** general elections, in **May 1952**.

:::cloze
The Indian Independence Act, 1947 made the Constituent Assembly a fully {{sovereign}} body and also gave it the role of a {{legislative|legislature}} body.
In its law-making role the Assembly was chaired by {{G.V. Mavalankar|Mavalankar}}, while in its constitution-making role it was chaired by {{Dr. Rajendra Prasad|Rajendra Prasad}}.
After the withdrawal of the Muslim League members from Pakistan areas, the strength of the Assembly fell from 389 to {{299}}.
:::

## Other work of the Assembly

The Assembly did far more than draft a constitution. It performed the functions
of a sovereign state in the making:

- Ratified India's membership of the **Commonwealth** in **May 1949**.
- Adopted the **national flag** on **22 July 1947**.
- Adopted the **national anthem** and the **national song** on
  **24 January 1950**.
- Elected **Dr. Rajendra Prasad** as the **first President of India** on
  **24 January 1950**.

Its working statistics are themselves a question bank. The Assembly held **11
sessions** over **2 years, 11 months and 18 days**. Its framers studied the
constitutions of about **60 countries**. The draft was under consideration for
**114 days**. The total expenditure was **₹64 lakh**.

> [!tip] Remember **24 January 1950** as a triple date — anthem, song, and the
> election of the first President — all on the Assembly's final sitting, when the
> members also signed the Constitution.

:::cloze
The national flag was adopted on {{22 July 1947}}, while the national anthem and the national song were adopted on {{24 January 1950}}.
The Assembly held {{11|eleven}} sessions spread over 2 years, {{11|eleven}} months and {{18|eighteen}} days, and the total expenditure came to about {{64 lakh|Rs 64 lakh|₹64 lakh}}.
:::

ones and their chairmen:

| Committee | Chairman |
|---|---|

| Union Constitution Committee | Jawaharlal Nehru |
| States Committee | Jawaharlal Nehru |
| Provincial Constitution Committee | Sardar Patel |

| **Drafting Committee** | **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** |

Notice the pattern: **Nehru chaired three, Patel two, Rajendra Prasad two,

### The Drafting Committee

Set up on **29 August 1947**, this was the most important of the lot. It had
**seven members**:

1. **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar (Chairman)**
2. N. Gopalaswamy Ayyangar
3. Alladi Krishnaswamy Ayyar
4. Dr. K.M. Munshi
5. Syed Mohammad Saadullah
6. N. Madhava Rau — replaced **B.L. Mitter**, who resigned
7. T.T. Krishnamachari — replaced **D.P. Khaitan**, who died in 1948

It prepared the **first draft**, published in **February 1948**, and gave the
public **eight months** to discuss and propose amendments. In the light of that
feedback it published a **second draft in October 1948**. For all its
importance, the committee sat for only **141 days**.

:::cloze
The Drafting Committee was set up on {{29 August 1947}} with {{7|seven}} members under the chairmanship of {{Dr. B.R. Ambedkar|B.R. Ambedkar|Ambedkar}}.
Its first draft was published in {{February 1948}} and the second draft in {{October 1948}}; the committee sat for only {{141}} days.
{{T.T. Krishnamachari}} replaced D.P. Khaitan, who died in 1948, and {{N. Madhava Rau}} replaced B.L. Mitter.
:::

## Enactment: the three readings

- **First reading.** Ambedkar introduced the final draft on **4 November 1948**,
  and general discussion on it ran to **9 November 1948**.
- **Second reading.** The clause-by-clause consideration ran from **15 November
  1948 to 17 October 1949** — the long grind. A total of **7,653 amendments**
  were proposed, of which **2,473** were actually discussed.
- **Third reading.** Begun on **14 November 1949**, it ended with the
  Constitution being **passed on 26 November 1949**. The motion was declared
  carried, and **284 of the 299 members** present signed it.

The Constitution as adopted contained a **Preamble, 395 Articles and 8
Schedules**. Note the drafting order: the **Preamble was enacted last**, after
the rest of the Constitution had been settled, so that it would state exactly
what the finished document did.

**Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**, then Law Minister and the man who piloted the draft
through the Assembly, is called the **"Father of the Constitution"** and the
**"Chief Architect of the Constitution"**. Since **2015**, **26 November** is
observed as **Constitution Day** (*Samvidhan Divas*).

> [!warn] **26 November 1949** is the date of **adoption**, which is why it
> appears in the Preamble ("this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949"). **26
> January 1950** is the date of **commencement**. Adoption ≠ commencement.

:::cloze
The Constitution was passed and adopted on {{26 November 1949}}, when {{284}} of the members present signed it.
As adopted, it contained a Preamble, {{395}} Articles and {{8|eight}} Schedules.
During the second reading, {{7653|7,653}} amendments were moved, of which {{2473|2,473}} were discussed.
:::

## Enforcement: the two dates

Some provisions could not wait for the Republic, because the machinery to create
the Republic had to exist first. So the Constitution came into force in **two
stages**:

- **26 November 1949** — the provisions on **citizenship, elections, the
  provisional Parliament, and temporary and transitional provisions** took
  effect immediately: **Articles 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 60, 324, 366, 367, 379, 380,
  388, 391, 392 and 393**.
- **26 January 1950** — the **remaining provisions** came into force. This is
  the **date of commencement** as defined in the Constitution itself, and it is
  celebrated as **Republic Day**. The date was chosen because the Congress, at
  its **Lahore session of December 1929**, had resolved to observe **26 January
  1930 as Purna Swaraj Day**.

With commencement, the **Indian Independence Act, 1947** and the **Government of
India Act, 1935** — together with all amendments and supplementary legislation —
were **repealed**. One statute survived the sweep: the **Abolition of Privy
Council Jurisdiction Act, 1949**.

:::cloze
The remaining provisions of the Constitution came into force on {{26 January 1950}}, known as the date of {{commencement}} and celebrated as {{Republic Day}}.
That date was chosen because the Congress had observed {{26 January 1930}} as {{Purna Swaraj}} Day, following its {{Lahore}} session of December 1929.
On commencement, the Indian Independence Act, 1947 and the {{Government of India Act, 1935}} stood repealed, but the {{Abolition of Privy Council Jurisdiction Act, 1949}} continued.
:::

## What the critics said

The Assembly has never been short of critics, and the exam expects you to know
the criticism by name and by author.

1. **Not a representative body.** Its members were not directly elected by the
   people of India on universal adult franchise.
2. **Not a sovereign body.** It was created by the proposals of the British
   government and met with its permission. (The Independence Act, 1947 answered
   this one.)
3. **Time-consuming.** It took **2 years, 11 months and 18 days**, where the
   framers of the American Constitution finished in under four months.
   **Naziruddin Ahmed** coined the jibe that the Drafting Committee should be
   called the **"Drifting Committee"**.
4. **Dominated by the Congress.** **Granville Austin** wrote that "the Assembly
   was the Congress and the Congress was India."
5. **Dominated by lawyers and politicians**, which is blamed for the bulk and
   the complicated language of the document.
6. **Dominated by Hindus.** **Lord Viscount Simon** called it "a body of
   Hindus"; **Winston Churchill** said it represented "only one major community
   in India".

The time criticism deserves a second look, because the comparison is unfair:

| Country | Articles | Time taken |
|---|---|---|
| U.S.A. | 7 | Under 4 months (1787) |
| South Africa | 153 | 1 year (1908–09) |
| Canada | 147 | About 2 years 6 months |
| India | 395 | 2 years 11 months 18 days |
| Australia | 128 | About 9 years (1891–1900) |

> [!note] India produced **395 Articles** in under three years while the American
> convention produced **7** in four months, and Australia took **nine years** for
> **128**. Measured per Article, the Indian Assembly was fast, not slow.

:::cloze

## The Assembly's own trivia

These are pure recall marks and appear regularly:

- The **elephant** was the **symbol / seal** of the Constituent Assembly.
- **Sir B.N. Rau** — constitutional (legal) adviser.
- **H.V.R. Iyengar** — Secretary to the Assembly.
- **S.N. Mukherjee** — Chief Draftsman.
- **Prem Behari Narain Raizada** — calligrapher; he **handwrote** the original
  in a flowing italic style. The Constitution was not printed or typed.
- The original was beautified by artists from **Shantiniketan**, led by
  **Nandalal Bose**. **Beohar Rammanohar Sinha** illuminated and decorated the
  **Preamble** page.
- The **Hindi** version was calligraphed by **Vasant Krishan Vaidya** and
  decorated by **Nandalal Bose**.

Also remember the Congress's own preparatory body: an **Experts Committee**
appointed on **8 July 1946** under **Nehru**, with members including M. Asaf Ali,
K.M. Munshi, N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, K.T. Shah, D.R. Gadgil, Humayun Kabir and
K. Santhanam. Granville Austin credits it with having "set India on the road to
her present Constitution" — the resolution it drafted closely resembled the
Objectives Resolution that Nehru would move five months later.

## The authoritative Hindi text

The Constitution was enacted in English, and originally there was **no provision
for an authoritative Hindi text**. That gap was filled by the **58th Amendment
Act, 1987**, which inserted **Article 394-A** in **Part XXII**. It provides that:

1. The **President** shall cause to be published a **Hindi translation** of the
   Constitution and of every amendment to it.
2. The Hindi translation shall be construed to have the **same meaning** as the
   English original; where a difficulty arises, the President shall cause the
   translation to be revised suitably.
3. The published Hindi translation shall be deemed to be the **authoritative
   text in Hindi**.

Four Articles sit together at the very end of the Constitution and are worth
memorising as a block: **Article 393** — short title; **394** — commencement;
**394-A** — authoritative Hindi text; **395** — repeals.

:::cloze
An authoritative Hindi text was provided for by the {{58th}} Amendment Act of {{1987}}, which inserted Article {{394-A|394A}} in Part XXII.
Of the closing Articles, {{393}} gives the short title, {{394}} the commencement and {{395}} the repeals.
:::

:::recall In one line — the Assembly was attacked as neither representative nor sovereign. Which charge did the Indian Independence Act, 1947 actually cure, and which one it could never cure?
It cured the **sovereignty** charge — from 1947 the Assembly could frame any constitution and abrogate any British law applying to India — but it could not cure the **representation** charge, because the members remained indirectly elected by provincial legislators rather than by the people on adult franchise.
:::
